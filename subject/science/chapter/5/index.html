🧪 Chapter 5: Exploring Mixtures and Their Separation

Verified step-by-step solutions for your class notes.

Q1
Which of the following mixtures are correctly classified as homogeneous (Hm) and heterogeneous (Ht)? Choose the correct option.
(i) Air — Hm, Milk — Ht, Sugar solution — Hm, Smoke — Hm
(ii) Brass — Ht, Fog — Ht, Vinegar — Ht, Muddy water — Hm
(iii) Copper sulfate solution — Hm, Salt solution — Hm, Milk — Hm, Bronze — Hm
(iv) Muddy water — Ht, Milk — Ht, Blood — Ht, Brass — Hm
Correct Option: (iv) Muddy water — Ht, Milk — Ht, Blood — Ht, Brass — Hm

Reason:
• Muddy water is a suspension, and milk and blood are colloids; they are heterogeneous (Ht) mixtures with non-uniform composition.
• Brass is an alloy (homogeneous mixture, Hm) of copper and zinc with uniform composition throughout.
Q2
Choose the correct options, and explain the reason for the correct and incorrect options.
Which among the following mixtures show the Tyndall Effect? A mixture of:
(a) air and dust particles
(b) copper sulfate and water
(c) starch and water
(d) acetone and water
Options -:
(i) a and b | (ii) b and d | (iii) a and c | (iv) c and d
Correct Option: (iii) a and c

Reason for correct options:
• (a) Air and dust particles and (c) Starch and water are colloidal systems. Their particles are large enough (1–1000 nm) to scatter a beam of light and make its path visible (Tyndall effect).

Reason for incorrect options:
• (b) Copper sulfate and water and (d) Acetone and water form true solutions. The solute particles are less than 1 nm in diameter, which is too small to scatter light, so they do not show the Tyndall effect.

Q4
Solve the following problems:
(i) A cake recipe uses dry ingredients, namely 75 g of sugar for 420 g of all-purpose flour and 5 g of sodium hydrogencarbonate. Express the concentration of each component in the mixture using an appropriate method.

(ii) A brass alloy contains 70% copper by mass. Calculate the quantities of copper and zinc present in 120 g of brass.
(i) Concentration of dry ingredients (% by mass):
Total mass of dry mixture = $75 ext{ g (sugar)} + 420 ext{ g (flour)} + 5 ext{ g (baking soda)} = 500 ext{ g}$

• Mass % of Sugar = $frac{75}{500} imes 100 = mathbf{15%}$
• Mass % of Flour = $frac{420}{500} imes 100 = mathbf{84%}$
• Mass % of Sodium hydrogencarbonate = $frac{5}{500} imes 100 = mathbf{1%}$

(ii) Quantities of copper and zinc in 120 g of brass:
Brass contains 70% Copper and 30% Zinc by mass.
• Mass of Copper = $70% ext{ of } 120 ext{ g} = frac{70}{100} imes 120 = mathbf{84 ext{ g}}$
• Mass of Zinc = $120 ext{ g} - 84 ext{ g} = mathbf{36 ext{ g}}$ (or $30% ext{ of } 120 ext{ g} = 36 ext{ g}$)
Q5
The label on a cooking oil pack says one litre (910 g). If this oil is mixed with water, will it form a separate layer? If so, which substance will be on top? How will you separate the two layers? Also, draw the diagram of the apparatus used.
1. Formation of separate layer:
Yes, cooking oil and water are immiscible liquids. When mixed together, they do not dissolve in each other and will form two distinct layers.

2. Substance on top:
Density of cooking oil = $frac{910 ext{ g}}{1000 ext{ mL}} = 0.91 ext{ g/cm}^3$, which is less than the density of water ($1.0 ext{ g/cm}^3$). Because oil is lighter (less dense), oil will float on top of water.

3. Method of separation:
The two layers can be separated using a separating funnel:
• Pour the mixture into a separating funnel and clamp it vertically on a stand. Allow it to stand undisturbed until the oil and water layers separate completely.
• Place a beaker below and open the stopcock to carefully drain out the lower water layer.
• Close the stopcock as soon as the oil interface reaches the opening, leaving only oil in the funnel.
Q6
Assertion (A): Solutions do not exhibit the Tyndall effect.

Reason (R): The particles in solutions are larger than 100 nm, so they cannot scatter light.
Choose the correct option:
(i) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
(ii) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
(iii) A is true, but R is false.
(iv) A is false, but R is true.
Correct Option: (iii) A is true, but R is false.

Reason:
• Assertion (A) is true: True solutions do not exhibit the Tyndall effect because their particles are too small to scatter light.
• Reason (R) is false: The particle size in a true solution is less than $1 ext{ nm}$ in diameter, not larger than $100 ext{ nm}$.

Q8
Two miscible liquids, A and B, are present in a mixture. The boiling point of A is 60 °C and the boiling point of B is 90 °C. Suggest a method to separate them. Also, draw a labelled diagram of the method suggested
Method of Separation: Simple Distillation

Reason:
Liquids A and B are miscible, and the difference in their boiling points is $90^circ ext{C} - 60^circ ext{C} = 30^circ ext{C}$, which is greater than $25^circ ext{C}$. Therefore, simple distillation can easily separate them without needing a fractionating column. Liquid A has a lower boiling point ($60^circ ext{C}$) and vaporises first. Its vapours pass through the condenser, condense into liquid, and collect in the receiver flask, while Liquid B ($90^circ ext{C}$) remains behind in the distillation flask.
Q9
Compare evaporation, crystallization and distillation. In which situation, would you prefer each of these over the others?
Comparison and Preferences:

• Evaporation: The process of converting a liquid into vapour by heating to recover the dissolved solid solute.
Preferred when: Only the non-volatile solid solute needs to be recovered and losing the solvent to the atmosphere is acceptable (e.g., obtaining common salt from sea water).

• Crystallisation: The process of cooling a hot, concentrated solution to obtain pure crystals of a solid.
Preferred over evaporation when: High purity is required, or when the solute decomposes, chars, or gets ruined upon heating to dryness (e.g., purifying copper sulphate, alum, or sugar).

• Distillation: The process of heating a liquid into vapour and then condensing the vapour back into liquid.
Preferred when: The liquid solvent itself needs to be recovered, or when separating two miscible liquids with a boiling point difference greater than $25^circ ext{C}$ (e.g., obtaining distilled water from saltwater, or separating water and acetone).
Q10
Blood is an example of a colloidal mixture

(i) What would happen if blood behaved like a true suspension inside the body?
(ii) In a blood sample, identify the dispersed phase and the dispersion medium.
(i) If blood behaved like a true suspension:
Suspensions are unstable and their particles settle down when left undisturbed. If blood were a suspension, blood cells (RBCs, WBCs, platelets) would settle at the bottom of blood vessels whenever the body is resting or still. This would block blood vessels, halt the continuous circulation of blood, and deprive vital organs of oxygen and nutrients.

(ii) Dispersed phase and Dispersion medium:
• Dispersed phase: Blood cells (Red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets).
• Dispersion medium: Liquid blood plasma.
Q11
You are given a mixture of sand, common salt and naphthalene (Fig. 5.25a). The Fig. 5.25b depicts various steps used to separate the components of this mixture. Identify and write down the correct sequence of separation techniques.
Correct Sequence of Separation: 3 → 1 → 2

Step-by-step Procedure:
1. Setup 3 (Sublimation): Heat the mixture in a china dish covered with an inverted funnel. Naphthalene is sublimable and changes directly into vapour. It deposits on the cold inner walls of the funnel, separating it from sand and salt.
2. Setup 1 (Dissolution and Filtration): Add water to the remaining sand and salt mixture and stir thoroughly. Common salt dissolves in water while sand remains insoluble. Filter the mixture; insoluble sand is collected as residue on the filter paper, and salt solution is obtained as filtrate.
3. Setup 2 (Evaporation): Heat the salt filtrate in a china dish until all the water evaporates, leaving behind dry common salt crystals.
Q12
Why is distillation an effective method for separating a mixture of water and acetone?
Distillation is an effective method because acetone and water are miscible liquids with a large difference in their boiling points ($> 25^circ ext{C}$):

• Boiling point of acetone = $56^circ ext{C}$
• Boiling point of water = $100^circ ext{C}$
• Difference in boiling points = $100^circ ext{C} - 56^circ ext{C} = 44^circ ext{C}$

When the mixture is heated in a distillation flask, the more volatile liquid (acetone) boils at $56^circ ext{C}$, vaporises first, and passes through the condenser where it condenses back into liquid and collects in the receiver. Water, having a higher boiling point of $100^circ ext{C}$, remains behind in the flask.
Q13
Answer the following questions with the help of the data given in Table 5.4
(i)What mass of potassium nitrate would be needed to prepare its saturated solution in 50 g of water at 40 °C?
(ii) A student makes a saturated solution of potassium chloride in water at 80 °C and leaves the solution to cool at room temperature (25 °C). What would she observe as the solution cools? Explain.
(iii) What is the effect of a change in temperature on the solubility of salts? Also, compare the changes in the solubility of the four given salts with increasing temperature from 10 °C to 80 °C.
(i) Mass of potassium nitrate needed:
From Table 5.4, the solubility of potassium nitrate ($ ext{KNO}_3$) at $40^circ ext{C}$ is $62 ext{ g}$ per $100 ext{ g}$ of water.
Therefore, in $50 ext{ g}$ of water, the mass needed = $frac{62}{100} imes 50 = mathbf{31 ext{ g}}$.

(ii) Observation on cooling:
Solid crystals of potassium chloride ($ ext{KCl}$) will separate out and deposit at the bottom of the beaker.
Reason: The solubility of potassium chloride decreases as temperature drops (from $54 ext{ g}$ per $100 ext{ g}$ of water at $80^circ ext{C}$ to approximately $35 ext{ g}$ at $25^circ ext{C}$). The excess dissolved salt ($54 ext{ g} - 35 ext{ g} = 19 ext{ g}$) can no longer remain in solution and crystallises out.

(iii) Effect of temperature on solubility:
• In general, the solubility of solid salts in water increases with an increase in temperature.
• Comparison ($10^circ ext{C}$ to $80^circ ext{C}$):
- Potassium nitrate: Shows the steepest increase in solubility, increasing dramatically from $21 ext{ g}$ to $167 ext{ g}$ per $100 ext{ g}$ of water.
- Potassium chloride and Ammonium chloride: Show a steady, substantial increase with temperature.
- Sodium chloride: Shows minimal change; its solubility remains nearly constant (increasing only slightly from $36 ext{ g}$ to $37 ext{ g}$).
Q14
Three students, A, B and C, are preparing sugar solutions for an experiment:

- Student A dissolves 20 g of sugar in 80 g of water.
- Student B dissolves 20 g of sugar in 100 g of water.
- Student C dissolves 30 g of sugar in 80 g of water.

(i) Calculate the mass percentage (% m/m) concentration of sugar in each student’s solution.
(ii) Whose solution is the most concentrated? Explain why.
(i) Mass percentage (% m/m) concentration:
$$ ext{Mass } % = frac{ ext{Mass of solute}}{ ext{Mass of solution}} imes 100$$
• Student A:
Mass of solution = $20 ext{ g} + 80 ext{ g} = 100 ext{ g}$
$$ ext{Concentration} = frac{20}{100} imes 100 = mathbf{20%}$$
• Student B:
Mass of solution = $20 ext{ g} + 100 ext{ g} = 120 ext{ g}$
$$ ext{Concentration} = frac{20}{120} imes 100 = mathbf{16.67%}$$
• Student C:
Mass of solution = $30 ext{ g} + 80 ext{ g} = 110 ext{ g}$
$$ ext{Concentration} = frac{30}{110} imes 100 = mathbf{27.27%}$$

(ii) Most concentrated solution:
Student C's solution is the most concentrated (27.27%). It contains the highest proportion of solute (sugar) relative to the total mass of the solution.

Every essential Class 9 Mathematics (Ganita Prakash) and Science (Physics & Chemistry) formula, algebraic identity, mensuration table, and SI unit in one place.

Maths • 01
Polynomials & All 10 Standard Algebraic Identities
  • Identity I (Square of a Binomial Sum): \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\)
  • Identity II (Square of a Binomial Difference): \((a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2\)
  • Identity III (Difference of Squares): \(a^2 - b^2 = (a + b)(a - b)\)
  • Identity IV (Product with Common Term): \((x + a)(x + b) = x^2 + (a + b)x + ab\)
  • Identity V (Square of a Trinomial): \((a + b + c)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca\)
  • Identity VI (Cube of a Sum): \((a + b)^3 = a^3 + b^3 + 3ab(a + b) = a^3 + 3a^2b + 3ab^2 + b^3\)
  • Identity VII (Cube of a Difference): \((a - b)^3 = a^3 - b^3 - 3ab(a - b) = a^3 - 3a^2b + 3ab^2 - b^3\)
  • Identity VIII (Sum of Cubes): \(a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)\)
  • Identity IX (Difference of Cubes): \(a^3 - b^3 = (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)\)
  • Identity X (Gauss / Trinomial Cube Identity): \(a^3 + b^3 + c^3 - 3abc = (a + b + c)(a^2 + b^2 + c^2 - ab - bc - ca)\)
  • Special Exam Shortcut: If \(a + b + c = 0\), then \(a^3 + b^3 + c^3 = 3abc\).
  • Remainder Theorem: When a polynomial \(p(x)\) of degree \(\ge 1\) is divided by \((x - a)\), the remainder is \(p(a)\). If \(p(a) = 0\), then \((x - a)\) is a factor of \(p(x)\).

Maths • 03
Coordinate Geometry & Linear Equations in Two Variables
  • Quadrant Sign Rules \((x, y)\): Quadrant I: \((+, +)\) • Quadrant II: \((-, +)\) • Quadrant III: \((-, -)\) • Quadrant IV: \((+, -)\).
  • Points on Axes: Any point on the \(x\)-axis is \((x, 0)\); any point on the \(y\)-axis is \((0, y)\); the Origin is \((0, 0)\).
  • Distance Formula: Distance between \(P(x_1, y_1)\) and \(Q(x_2, y_2)\) is \(PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\).
  • Distance from Origin: Distance of \(P(x, y)\) from \((0, 0)\) is \(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\).
  • Midpoint Formula: The midpoint of segment joining \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\) is \(M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)\).
  • Standard Linear Equation in Two Variables: \(ax + by + c = 0\) (where \(a, b\) are not both zero). Its graph is a straight line with infinitely many solutions.
  • Slope-Intercept Form: \(y = mx + c\), where \(m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\) is the slope (rate of change) and \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept.
Maths • 04
Number Systems, Laws of Exponents & Surds
  • Product & Quotient of Powers: \(a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}\)   and   \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\) (\(a \ne 0\)).
  • Power of a Power & Product: \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\)   and   \((ab)^m = a^m b^m\).
  • Zero, Negative & Fractional Exponents: \(a^0 = 1\),   \(a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}\),   \(a^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^m} = (\sqrt[n]{a})^m\).
  • Laws of Radicals (Surds): \(\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}\),   \(\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}\),   \((\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}) = a - b\).
  • Rationalising the Denominator: Multiply numerator and denominator of \(\frac{1}{a + b\sqrt{x}}\) by its conjugate \((a - b\sqrt{x})\) so the denominator becomes \(a^2 - b^2x\).
Maths • 05
2D Perimeter, Area, Heron's Formula & Polygon Geometry
  • Baudhāyana-Pythagoras Theorem: In a right-angled triangle with legs \(a, b\) and hypotenuse \(c\): \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\).
  • Heron's Formula (Any Triangle with sides \(a, b, c\)): Semi-perimeter \(s = \frac{a + b + c}{2}\);   \(\text{Area} = \sqrt{s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}\).
  • Equilateral Triangle (side \(a\)): \(\text{Area} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2\),   Altitude \(h = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a\),   Perimeter \(= 3a\).
  • Quadrilaterals: Parallelogram Area \(= \text{base} \times \text{height}\);   Rhombus Area \(= \frac{1}{2} \times d_1 \times d_2\);   Trapezium Area \(= \frac{1}{2}(a + b) \times h\).
  • Circle, Sector & Ring: Circumference \(= 2\pi r\),   Area \(= \pi r^2\),   Annulus (Ring) Area \(= \pi(R^2 - r^2)\),   Sector Area \(= \frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\pi r^2\).
  • Polygons (\(n\) sides): Sum of interior angles \(= (n - 2) \times 180^\circ\);   Sum of exterior angles \(= 360^\circ\);   Number of diagonals \(= \frac{n(n - 3)}{2}\).
Maths • 06
Probability, Statistics (Data) & Number Sequences
  • Empirical / Classical Probability: \(P(E) = \frac{\text{Number of outcomes favourable to } E}{\text{Total number of possible outcomes}}\), where \(0 \le P(E) \le 1\).
  • Complementary Event: \(P(\text{not } E) = P(\bar{E}) = 1 - P(E)\).
  • Arithmetic Mean (Average): \(\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}\)   (or for frequency table: \(\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}\)).
  • Median (Sorted Data of size \(n\)): If \(n\) is odd, \(\text{Median} = \left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}}\) term;   if \(n\) is even, \(\text{Median} = \text{Average of } \left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{ and } \left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)^{\text{th}}\) terms.
  • Mode & Range: Mode \(=\) most frequently occurring observation;   \(\text{Range} = \text{Maximum Value} - \text{Minimum Value}\).
  • Linear / Arithmetic Sequence: \(n^{\text{th}}\) term \(T_n = a + (n - 1)d\);   Sum of first \(n\) natural numbers \(= \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}\);   Sum of first \(n\) odd numbers \(= n^2\).
Science • 01
Physics: Describing Motion & Three Equations of Motion
  • Speed & Velocity: \(\text{Speed} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}}\) (Scalar, \(\text{m/s}\));   \(\text{Velocity} = \frac{\text{Displacement}}{\text{Time}}\) (Vector, \(\text{m/s}\)).
  • Conversion Shortcut: \(\text{km/h} \xrightarrow{\times \frac{5}{18}} \text{m/s}\)   and   \(\text{m/s} \xrightarrow{\times \frac{18}{5}} \text{km/h}\).
  • Average Speed & Uniform Acceleration Velocity: \(v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}}\);   for uniform acceleration, \(v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{u + v}{2}\).
  • Acceleration: \(a = \frac{v - u}{t}\)   (SI Unit: \(\text{m/s}^2\); negative acceleration is retardation/deceleration).
  • 1st Equation of Motion (Velocity-Time): \(v = u + at\)
  • 2nd Equation of Motion (Position-Time): \(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2\)
  • 3rd Equation of Motion (Position-Velocity): \(v^2 - u^2 = 2as\)   (or \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\))
  • Uniform Circular Motion Speed: \(v = \frac{2\pi r}{t}\) (speed is constant, velocity changes continuously due to changing direction).
Science • 02
Physics: Force, Laws of Motion, Gravitation & Pressure
  • Linear Momentum: \(p = m \times v\)   (SI Unit: \(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}\)).
  • Newton's Second Law of Motion: \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{t} = \frac{m(v - u)}{t} = m \times a\)   (SI Unit: \(\text{Newton (N)} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2\)).
  • Impulse: \(J = F \times t = m(v - u) = \Delta p\) (Change in momentum).
  • Law of Conservation of Momentum: \(m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2\)   (Recoil velocity of gun: \(V = -\frac{mv}{M}\)).
  • Universal Law of Gravitation: \(F = G\frac{M \cdot m}{d^2}\), where \(G = 6.673 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2\).
  • Acceleration due to Gravity & Weight: \(g = \frac{GM}{R^2} \approx 9.8\text{ m/s}^2\);   \(\text{Weight } W = m \times g\);   \(W_{\text{Moon}} = \frac{1}{6}W_{\text{Earth}}\).
  • Free Fall Equations (downward \(+g\), upward \(-g\)): \(v = u + gt\),   \(h = ut + \frac{1}{2}gt^2\),   \(v^2 = u^2 + 2gh\).
  • Pressure, Density & Buoyancy: \(P = \frac{\text{Thrust (F)}}{\text{Area (A)}}\) (\(\text{Pa} = \text{N/m}^2\));   \(\text{Density } \rho = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}\) (\(\text{kg/m}^3\));   \(\text{Relative Density} = \frac{\rho_{\text{substance}}}{\rho_{\text{water}}}\) (No unit).
Science • 03
Physics: Work, Energy, Power & Simple Machines
  • Work Done by a Constant Force: \(W = F \times s\) (when force is along displacement; \(W = 0\) if \(\theta = 90^\circ\) or \(s = 0\)). SI Unit: \(\text{Joule (J)} = 1\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\).
  • Kinetic Energy: \(E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m}\);   Work-Energy Theorem: \(W = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mu^2\).
  • Gravitational Potential Energy: \(E_p = m \times g \times h\).
  • Law of Conservation of Mechanical Energy: \(E_k + E_p = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + mgh = \text{Constant}\).
  • Power: \(P = \frac{\text{Work (W)}}{\text{Time (t)}} = F \times v\)   (SI Unit: \(\text{Watt (W)} = \text{J/s}\);   \(1\text{ kW} = 1000\text{ W}\),   \(1\text{ hp} \approx 746\text{ W}\)).
  • Commercial Unit of Electrical Energy: \(1\text{ Unit} = 1\text{ kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ Joules}\).
  • Simple Machines & Levers: \(\text{Mechanical Advantage (MA)} = \frac{\text{Load (L)}}{\text{Effort (E)}} = \frac{\text{Effort Arm}}{\text{Load Arm}}\);   \(\text{Efficiency } \eta = \frac{\text{Work Output}}{\text{Work Input}} \times 100\%\).
Science • 04
Physics: Sound Waves, Frequency, Echo & SONAR
  • Frequency & Time Period Relation: \(\nu = \frac{1}{T}\) and \(T = \frac{1}{\nu}\)   (Frequency SI Unit: \(\text{Hertz (Hz)} = \text{s}^{-1}\)).
  • Wave Velocity Equation: \(v = \lambda \times \nu = \frac{\lambda}{T}\)   (where \(v =\) wave speed, \(\lambda =\) wavelength, \(\nu =\) frequency).
  • Echo & SONAR Ranging Formula: Total distance travelled by sound \(= 2d = v \times t \implies d = \frac{v \times t}{2}\).
  • Minimum Distance for Distinct Echo in Air: Since persistence of hearing is \(0.1\text{ s}\) and \(v_{\text{air}} \approx 344\text{ m/s}\) at \(22^\circ\text{C}\), \(d_{\min} = \frac{344 \times 0.1}{2} = 17.2\text{ m}\).
  • Frequency Ranges: Infrasonic: \(< 20\text{ Hz}\) • Human Audible Range: \(20\text{ Hz}\text{ to }20,000\text{ Hz}\) (\(20\text{ kHz}\)) • Ultrasonic: \(> 20\text{ kHz}\).
Science • 05
Chemistry: Solution Concentration, Atomic Structure & Mole Concept
  • Mass by Mass % of a Solution: \(\frac{\text{Mass of Solute}}{\text{Mass of Solution}} \times 100 = \frac{\text{Mass of Solute}}{\text{Mass of Solute} + \text{Mass of Solvent}} \times 100\).
  • Mass by Volume % of a Solution: \(\frac{\text{Mass of Solute}}{\text{Volume of Solution}} \times 100\).
  • Volume by Volume % of a Solution: \(\frac{\text{Volume of Solute}}{\text{Volume of Solution}} \times 100\).
  • Temperature Scale Conversions: \(T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15\)   and   \(^\circ\text{F} = \frac{9}{5}(^\circ\text{C}) + 32\).
  • Atomic Number (\(Z\)) & Mass Number (\(A\)): \(Z = \text{Number of protons } (p^+)\);   \(A = \text{Protons } (Z) + \text{Neutrons } (n^0) \implies n^0 = A - Z\).
  • Bohr-Bury Shell Capacity Rule: Maximum electrons in orbit \(n = 2n^2\)   (\(K=2,\; L=8,\; M=18,\; N=32\); outermost shell holds at most \(8\) electrons).
  • Average Atomic Mass of Isotopes: \(M_{\text{avg}} = \frac{(M_1 \times p_1) + (M_2 \times p_2)}{100}\)   (e.g., Chlorine: \(35 \times \frac{75}{100} + 37 \times \frac{25}{100} = 35.5\text{ u}\)).
  • Mole & Avogadro's Constant: \(\text{Number of Moles } (n) = \frac{\text{Given Mass } (m)}{\text{Molar Mass } (M)} = \frac{\text{Number of Particles } (N)}{N_A}\), where \(N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}\).
