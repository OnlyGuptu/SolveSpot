(ii) Phloem

Q3
You can perform these two jumps (Fig. 3.21):
Straight-leg jump — keep knees and ankles stiff.
Normal jump — bend knees and ankles naturally.
How did your ankle, knee and hip positions differ between the two jumps?
In a straight-leg jump, the hip, knee, and ankle joints remain stiff and straight, making it hard to push off the ground and causing a harsh landing. In a normal jump, the hip, knee, and ankle joints bend before pushing off and bend again upon landing. This helps generate a higher jump and absorbs shock smoothly when landing.

Q5
In each of the following cases (A, B, C and D), choose the correct option as given below:
(i) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
(ii) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
(iii) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
(iv) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

A. Assertion: Epithelium is well-suited for gas exchange in the lungs.
Reason: It consists of multiple layers of tall cells that slow down diffusion.

B. Assertion: Cardiac muscle can contract continuously without fatigue.
Reason: Cardiac muscle cells have a high number of mitochondria and an abundant blood supply.

C. Assertion: Tendons connect bone to bone and allow joint movement.
Reason: Tendons are made of tough connective tissue that transmits force from muscle to bone.

D. Assertion: In a hinge joint, movement occurs primarily in one plane.
Reason: The bone ends are shaped to allow sliding in all directions.
A: (iii) (A) is true, but (R) is false. (Alveoli in lungs are lined by a single thin layer of squamous epithelium for easy diffusion.)

B: (i) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

C: (iv) (A) is false, but (R) is true. (Ligaments connect bone to bone, whereas tendons connect muscle to bone.)

D: (iii) (A) is true, but (R) is false.

(i) Analyse the graph in terms of the diameter of the stem over time and share the interpretation.

(i) Graph Analysis:
The graph shows that as the age of the teak tree increases, the diameter of its stem (girth) also increases steadily over time.

(ii) Relation between diameter and annual rings:
The diameter of the tree is directly proportional to the number of annual rings formed. As a new ring is added each year, the thickness (diameter) of the trunk increases.

Q7
Look at the given figure showing the debarked portion of a tree trunk. Answer the following questions based on the image:

(i) What is the immediate impact of removing the bark on the tree's defense?
(ii) Which tissue lies just below the bark, and how does its damage affect the plant?
(iii) What will happen to the overall growth and survival of the tree over time?
(iv) What assumptions are made regarding the extent of the damage?
(i) Immediate impact on defense:
Bark is made up of dead cork cells filled with suberin, which forms an outer protective covering. Removing the bark exposes the inner tissues of the tree to mechanical injury, fungal or bacterial infections, insect attack, and excessive water loss by evaporation.

(ii) Tissue below the bark and effect of its damage:
The phloem (along with cambium) lies just below the outer bark. Phloem translocates prepared food from the leaves to the roots and other parts of the tree. If it is damaged, food supply to the roots is cut off.

(iii) Effect on overall growth and survival:
If a complete ring of bark and phloem is removed around the trunk, food cannot reach the roots. Over time, the roots will starve and die first, stop absorbing water and minerals, and eventually the entire tree will die.

(iv) Assumptions made:
We assume that the bark has been removed in a complete ring around the trunk (girdling) and deep enough to destroy the phloem and vascular cambium. If only a small patch of bark is removed on one side, food can still pass through the remaining intact phloem and the tree can heal and survive.
Q8
Aamrapali observed that a young mango sapling’s stem bends flexibly during monsoon winds and does not break. Which tissue is responsible for this flexibility? Predict and provide your explanation of the impact if the existing tissue was replaced by sclerenchyma.
The tissue responsible for flexibility in the young mango stem is collenchyma. Its cells are living, elongated, and unevenly thickened at the corners, which allows young stems and leaf stalks to bend easily in strong winds without snapping.

If replaced by sclerenchyma:
Sclerenchyma is made of dead cells with thick, hard, lignified walls that make plant parts rigid and stiff. If collenchyma in a young sapling were replaced by sclerenchyma, the stem would lose its flexibility, become brittle, and easily break during strong monsoon winds.
Q9
Sohan designed an experiment for the regeneration of sugarcane, where he used cuttings to grow sugarcane. He used two types of cuttings, type ‘A’ and type ‘B’ (Fig. 3.23). After a few weeks, type ‘B’ cuttings sprouted and developed into sugarcane plants, whereas the type ‘A’ cuttings did not sprout.

(i) Why were the type ‘B’ cuttings able to grow as sugarcane but type ‘A’ could not?
(ii) What difference was present in type ‘B’ compared to type ‘A’?
(iii) What observation or measurement was made to determine whether this change had an effect?
(iv) What parameters should be kept the same for both types of cuttings to ensure a fair comparison?
(i) Type ‘B’ cuttings grew into new plants because they had nodes with axillary buds containing intercalary/apical meristematic tissue. These cells divide actively to form new shoots and roots. Type ‘A’ cuttings did not have nodes, so they could not sprout.

(ii) Type ‘B’ cuttings had a node (joint with a bud), whereas Type ‘A’ cuttings had only the internode portion without any node.

(iii) Sohan observed whether new roots and leafy shoots sprouted from the cuttings after a few weeks.

(iv) Parameters to be kept the same:
• Type of soil and amount of manure
• Amount and frequency of watering
• Sunlight and temperature conditions
• Size and freshness of the sugarcane cuttings
Q10
During the discussion in class, Rohan gives a statement that, “A tissue is a group of similar cells performing similar functions”. But Rajiv counter argues that, “this is true in case of simple tissues but little different in case of complex tissues”. Provide your explanation in view of the discussion in class.
Both Rohan and Rajiv have valid points:

• Simple permanent tissues (like parenchyma, collenchyma, and sclerenchyma) are made up of only one type of cells that look alike and perform the same function, which matches Rohan's statement.
• Complex permanent tissues (like xylem and phloem) are made up of more than one type of cells (for example, xylem has tracheids, vessels, xylem parenchyma, and xylem fibres). Although these cells look different, they coordinate to perform a common function (conduction of water or food), which supports Rajiv's argument.

Therefore, a tissue is best defined as a group of similar or dissimilar cells that have a common origin and work together to perform a specific function.
Q11
Coconut husk fibres are used for mats which are tough and fibrous. Which tissue has structural features suitable for providing this strength? Explain why living parenchyma couldn’t serve the same purpose.
Coconut husk (coir) is made of sclerenchyma tissue. Its cells are dead, long, narrow, and have very thick walls due to the deposition of lignin, with almost no intercellular spaces. This gives the fibres high strength, stiffness, and durability suitable for making mats and ropes.

Why parenchyma cannot serve this purpose:
Parenchyma consists of living cells with thin cellulose cell walls and large intercellular spaces. It is soft and meant for storing food and water, so it lacks the toughness and tensile strength needed to make durable mats.
Q12
Vibha claims to her friend Neha that, “Meristematic cells are located only at the root and shoot apices”. What do you think about this statement? What question can Neha ask Vibha to help her understand further if the statement is incorrect?
Vibha's statement is incorrect. Meristematic tissue is not restricted only to the root and shoot tips (apical meristem); it is also present at the base of leaves and nodes (intercalary meristem) and along the sides of stems and roots (lateral meristem / cambium).

Question Neha can ask:
“If meristematic cells are only at the tips of roots and shoots, how does a tree trunk grow thicker every year, or how does grass grow back after its top tips are mowed or eaten by cattle?”
Q13
A plant cell and an animal cell are of the same size.
(i) Which cell will have a larger vacuole? Give reasons.
(ii) What assumptions are you making to answer the question above?
(i) The plant cell will have a much larger vacuole.
Reason: Mature plant cells have a large central vacuole (taking up 50% to 90% of the cell volume) filled with cell sap, which stores water and nutrients and provides turgidity and rigidity to the cell. Animal cells have either very small, temporary vacuoles or none at all.

(ii) Assumptions made:
• The plant cell is a mature, differentiated cell (since young meristematic plant cells have very small vacuoles or lack them).
• Both cells are normal, healthy cells in their natural state.
Q14
A textbook states, “Each plant tissue performs only one specific function”. What questions would you ask to critically examine the correctness of this statement? What examples of tissues would you take to find out the answers to these questions?
Questions to examine the statement:
1. Can a single type of plant tissue perform more than one function depending on where it is located?
2. Does a tissue that provides mechanical support also help in photosynthesis or storage?
3. Does a conducting tissue like xylem only transport water, or does it also give mechanical strength to the plant?

Examples of tissues:
• Parenchyma: Normally stores food, but when it contains chlorophyll (chlorenchyma) it performs photosynthesis, and in aquatic plants (aerenchyma) it traps air to help the plant float.
• Collenchyma: Provides flexibility and mechanical support to young stems, and also prepares food when chloroplasts are present.
• Xylem: Conducts water and minerals upward and also provides mechanical strength to the stem through thick-walled tracheids, vessels, and xylem fibres.
