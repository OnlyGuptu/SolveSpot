Chapter 9: Propositions and their Converses — Complete Questions and Answers (17 Q&As)

Q1

If two lines are parallel, then the corresponding angles formed by a transversal are equal.

Converse: If the corresponding angles formed by a transversal cutting two lines are equal, then the two lines are parallel.

Truth Value: Both the given statement and its converse are True (by the corresponding angles axiom for parallel lines).

Parallel lines l ∥ m cut by transversal t ⇔ Corresponding angles ∠1 = ∠2
Q2

If a quadrilateral is a square, then all its angles are equal.

Converse: If all the angles of a quadrilateral are equal, then it is a square.

Truth Value: The given proposition is True (every angle of a square is $90^\circ$). Its converse is False.

Counterexample: Take a rectangle whose length and breadth are different. All four of its angles are equal ($90^\circ$ each), yet it is not a square because all four sides are not equal.

Counterexample to Converse: A rectangle has all angles equal (90°) but is not a square
Q3

Given any $\Delta ABC$, let us bisect the angles at $B$ and $C$. The bisectors meet at the incentre $I$ of the triangle. Now extend the bisectors beyond $I$ till they meet the opposite sides at $E$ and $F$ respectively (as shown in Fig. 9.1).

Proposition: If $AB = AC$, then $IE = IF$.

Converse: If $IE = IF$, then $AB = AC$.

Truth Value: Both the proposition and its converse are True.

Proof of the Proposition: Since $AB = AC$, we have $\angle B = \angle C$ (angles opposite to equal sides). Because $BE$ and $CF$ bisect $\angle B$ and $\angle C$, we get $\angle IBC = \frac{1}{2}\angle B = \frac{1}{2}\angle C = \angle ICB$. So in $\Delta IBC$, the sides opposite to these equal angles are also equal: $IB = IC$.
Now, in $\Delta IBF$ and $\Delta ICE$:

  • $\angle IBF = \angle ICE$ (both are equal to $\frac{1}{2}\angle B = \frac{1}{2}\angle C$)
  • $IB = IC$ (proved above)
  • $\angle FIB = \angle EIC$ (vertically opposite angles)
By the ASA congruence rule, $\Delta IBF \cong \Delta ICE$. Hence, by CPCT, $IF = IE$ (or $IE = IF$).

Q4

If $x = y$, then $a + x = a + y$, where $x$, $y$ and $a$ are any three numbers.

Converse: If $a + x = a + y$, then $x = y$.

Truth Value: Both the proposition and its converse are True.

Reason: If two numbers are equal, adding the same number $a$ to both sides keeps them equal. Conversely, if $a + x = a + y$, subtracting $a$ from both sides gives $(a + x) - a = (a + y) - a$, which simplifies to $x = y$.

Q5

If $a$ and $b$ are perfect squares, then $ab$ is a perfect square.

Converse: If $ab$ is a perfect square, then $a$ and $b$ are perfect squares.

Truth Value: The proposition is True (if $a = m^2$ and $b = n^2$, then $ab = (mn)^2$), but the converse is False.

Counterexample: Take $a = 2$ and $b = 8$. Their product is $ab = 2 \times 8 = 16 = 4^2$, which is a perfect square, even though neither $2$ nor $8$ is a perfect square.

Q6

If $x = y$, then $x^2 = y^2$.

Converse: If $x^2 = y^2$, then $x = y$.

Truth Value: The proposition is True, but its converse is False.

Counterexample: Let $x = 3$ and $y = -3$. Then $x^2 = 3^2 = 9$ and $y^2 = (-3)^2 = 9$, so $x^2 = y^2$ holds true, but $x \neq y$ since $3 \neq -3$.

Q7

If $x = y$, then $x^3 = y^3$.

Converse: If $x^3 = y^3$, then $x = y$.

Truth Value: Both the proposition and its converse are True.

Reason: Every real number has only one real cube root (positive numbers have positive cube roots, and negative numbers have negative cube roots). So if $x^3 = y^3$, then $x$ must equal $y$.

Q8

If $n$ is divisible by 24, then it is divisible by both 4 and 6.

Converse: If $n$ is divisible by both 4 and 6, then it is divisible by 24.

Truth Value: The proposition is True, while its converse is False.

Counterexample: Take $n = 12$. Here, 12 is divisible by both 4 ($12 = 4 \times 3$) and 6 ($12 = 6 \times 2$), but 12 is not divisible by 24.
Why this happens: Since 4 and 6 share a common factor of 2, a number divisible by both 4 and 6 only needs to be a multiple of their LCM, which is $\text{lcm}(4, 6) = 12$, and not necessarily 24.

Q9

If $n$ is divisible by 60, then it is divisible by both 5 and 12.

Converse: If $n$ is divisible by both 5 and 12, then it is divisible by 60.

Truth Value: Both the proposition and its converse are True.

Reason for Converse: The numbers 5 and 12 are coprime (their highest common factor is 1). So any number that is a multiple of both 5 and 12 must be a multiple of their LCM: $$\text{lcm}(5, 12) = 5 \times 12 = 60$$

Q10

If $n$ is the square of a prime number, then it has exactly 3 factors.

Converse: If $n$ has exactly 3 factors, then it is the square of a prime number.

Truth Value: Both the proposition and its converse are True.

Reason: If $n = p^2$ for a prime number $p$, its only factors are $1$, $p$, and $p^2$ — so it has 3 factors.
Conversely, a number has an odd number of factors only when it is a perfect square. If $n$ has 3 factors, they must be $1$, $\sqrt{n}$, and $n$. If $\sqrt{n}$ were composite, its factors would also divide $n$, giving $n$ more than 3 factors. Therefore, $\sqrt{n}$ must be a prime number $p$, meaning $n = p^2$.

Q11

If $n$ is a product of two unequal prime numbers, then it has exactly 4 divisors.

Converse: If $n$ has exactly 4 divisors, then it is a product of two unequal prime numbers.

Truth Value: The proposition is True (if $n = pq$ for distinct primes $p, q$, its divisors are $1, p, q, pq$). The converse is False.

Counterexample: Take $n = 8$. Its divisors are $1, 2, 4,$ and $8$ (4 divisors in total). However, 8 is not a product of two unequal primes; it is the cube of a single prime ($8 = 2^3$).

Q12

If $n$ and $n+3$ have no factors in common, then $n$ is not a multiple of 3.

Converse: If $n$ is not a multiple of 3, then $n$ and $n+3$ have no common factors (other than 1).

Truth Value: Both the proposition and its converse are True.

Reason: Any common factor of $n$ and $n+3$ must also divide their difference: $$(n + 3) - n = 3$$ Since the only factors of 3 are 1 and 3, the only common factor $n$ and $n+3$ could possibly share (besides 1) is 3. So if $n$ is not a multiple of 3, then 3 does not divide $n$, which means $n$ and $n+3$ have no common factor greater than 1.

Q13

Find counterexamples to the following claims:
(i) All numbers of the form $4n^2 + 1$ are prime.
(ii) All numbers of the form $n^2 + n + 11$ are prime.
(iii) All numbers of the form $4^n + 3$ are prime.

  • (i) Put $n = 4$: $$4(4^2) + 1 = 4(16) + 1 = 64 + 1 = 65$$ Since $65 = 5 \times 13$, it is not prime.
  • (ii) Put $n = 11$: $$11^2 + 11 + 11 = 121 + 22 = 143$$ Since $143 = 11 \times 13$, it is not prime.
    (You can also take $n = 10$, which gives $10^2 + 10 + 11 = 121 = 11 \times 11$).
  • (iii) Put $n = 4$: $$4^4 + 3 = 256 + 3 = 259$$ Since $259 = 7 \times 37$, it is not prime.
Q14

Find counterexamples to the following statements:
(i) If $n$ is a prime number, then $2^n - 1$ is a prime number.
(ii) If $n$ is an even number, then $2^n + 1$ is a prime number.

  • (i) Take the prime number $n = 11$: $$2^{11} - 1 = 2048 - 1 = 2047$$ Here, $2047 = 23 \times 89$, so it is not prime.
  • (ii) Take the even number $n = 6$: $$2^6 + 1 = 64 + 1 = 65$$ Here, $65 = 5 \times 13$, so it is not prime.
    (Another example: for $n = 10$, $2^{10} + 1 = 1025 = 5^2 \times 41$).
Q15

Consider the statement: "If a number is divisible by 8, then it is divisible by both 2 and 4."

(i) Justify the statement.
(ii) Recall the divisibility shortcuts that we have studied for different numbers. To check whether a given number is divisible by 8, is it enough to check whether it is divisible by 2 and 4? Why or why not?

  • (i) Justification: Since $8 = 2 \times 4$, both 2 and 4 are factors of 8. Any number divisible by 8 can be written as $8k$, which is equal to $2(4k)$ as well as $4(2k)$. So it is always divisible by both 2 and 4.
  • (ii) Shortcut Check: No, checking divisibility by 2 and 4 is not enough to guarantee that a number is divisible by 8.

    Reason: That would rely on the converse statement ("If a number is divisible by both 2 and 4, then it is divisible by 8"), which is False.
    Counterexample: Take 12. It is divisible by both 2 and 4, but 12 is not divisible by 8. Because 2 is already a factor of 4, any multiple of 4 is automatically divisible by both 2 and 4, even if it is not a multiple of 8.
Q16

Recall that a shortcut to check whether a given number is divisible by 3 is to add the digits of the number and check if the sum is a multiple of 3. Express the relationship between 'a number is divisible by 3' and 'sum of the digits is a multiple of 3' using 'If-then' sentences.

We can write this relationship as a pair of 'If-then' statements (a proposition and its converse), both of which are true:

1. Proposition: "If a number is divisible by 3, then the sum of its digits is a multiple of 3."

2. Converse: "If the sum of the digits of a number is a multiple of 3, then the number is divisible by 3."

Since both statements work in either direction, we can combine them using "if and only if": A number is divisible by 3 if and only if the sum of its digits is a multiple of 3.

Q17

We have identified different types of quadrilaterals... Suppose we have identified a category of quadrilaterals called $Q$, and we have to construct a quadrilateral of this type. For this, we are to use two thin sticks, put them together as diagonals so that the quadrilateral obtained by joining their endpoints is of type $Q$ (see Fig. 9.2).

  • (i) Suppose $Q$ satisfies the following property: If a quadrilateral is of type $Q$, then it has equal-length diagonals.
    (a) Should the two sticks be of equal length? Why or why not?
    (b) Will it matter how the two sticks are put together?
  • (ii) Instead of the property mentioned above, suppose $Q$ satisfies the following property: If a quadrilateral has equal diagonals, then it is of type $Q$.
    What will be your answers to (a) and (b) now?
  • (i) Given property: "If a quadrilateral is of type $Q$, then it has equal-length diagonals."
    • (a) Should the two sticks be of equal length?
      Yes. Every quadrilateral in category $Q$ must have equal diagonals. If we start with sticks of different lengths, the resulting quadrilateral cannot possibly belong to type $Q$.
    • (b) Will it matter how the two sticks are put together?
      Yes, it can matter. The property only tells us that equal diagonals are required for type $Q$, not that every quadrilateral with equal diagonals is of type $Q$. For example, if $Q$ stands for rectangles, the two equal sticks must also bisect each other at their midpoints.
  • (ii) Given property: "If a quadrilateral has equal diagonals, then it is of type $Q$."
    • (a) Should the two sticks be of equal length?
      Yes. Taking two sticks of equal length satisfies the 'if' part of the rule, which guarantees that our quadrilateral will be of type $Q$.
    • (b) Will it matter how the two sticks are put together?
      No, it will not matter. The statement says that any quadrilateral with equal diagonals is automatically of type $Q$, no matter where or at what angle the two sticks cross each other.
