Chapter 12: Quadrilaterals — Complete Questions and Answers (19 Q&As)

Q1

Let ABCD be a quadrilateral.

  • (i) List all sides of ABCD adjacent to side AB. List all sides opposite to AB.
  • (ii) List all angles of ABCD adjacent to ∠A. List all angles opposite to ∠A.
  • (iii) Define a pair of opposite sides and a pair of opposite angles without using the names of the vertices.
  • (i) Adjacent to AB: Sides BC and DA. Opposite to AB: Side CD.
  • (ii) Adjacent to ∠A: Angles ∠B and ∠D. Opposite to ∠A: Angle ∠C.
  • (iii) Opposite sides are two sides of a quadrilateral that do not share a common vertex. Opposite angles are two internal angles that do not share a common side.
Q2

Precisely define the internal angle of a quadrilateral at a given vertex. Your answer should work for a non-convex quadrilateral too.

Answer: At any vertex of a quadrilateral, the two sides meeting there form an angle. The internal angle is the angle between these two sides that faces the inside of the quadrilateral. More precisely, if we pick two points on those two sides very close to the vertex, the angle between the sides that contains the line segment joining those two points inside the quadrilateral is the internal angle. In a non-convex quadrilateral (which has an inward dent), one of the internal angles is a reflex angle (greater than 180°).

Convex vs. Non-Convex quadrilateral showing internal reflex angle > 180°
Q3

In a quadrilateral ABCD, suppose AB || DC. Can ABCD be non-convex? What if we instead assume AB = CD? What if we instead assume ∠A = ∠C?

  • If AB || DC: No, ABCD cannot be non-convex (assuming it is not self-intersecting). Since AB || DC, the co-interior angles on each transversal add up to 180° (∠A + ∠D = 180° and ∠B + ∠C = 180°). Because no angle can be greater than 180°, the quadrilateral must be convex.
  • If AB = CD: Yes, it can be non-convex. We can easily draw a dart-shaped (non-convex) quadrilateral in which a pair of opposite sides are equal.
  • If ∠A = ∠C: Yes, it can be non-convex. A symmetric dart-shaped quadrilateral has its two non-reflex opposite angles (∠A and ∠C) equal.
Q4

Consider three non-collinear points A, B, C and draw the lines AB, BC, CA. For every possible location of point D in the plane outside these lines, decide if ABCD is self-intersecting, non-convex, or convex.

Answer: The three lines AB, BC, and CA divide the plane into 7 regions (the triangle ABC in the middle and 6 outer regions). Depending on which region point D lies in, joining A-B-C-D in order gives different types of quadrilaterals:

  • If D lies inside ΔABC, or in the two outer wedge regions across side AB or side BC, ABCD is non-convex (dart-shaped).
  • If D lies in the outer region across side AC (opposite to vertex B), ABCD is a convex quadrilateral.
  • If D lies in any of the three corner regions directly behind vertices A, B, or C, the sides cross each other and ABCD is a self-intersecting quadrilateral.
Q1

1. True or false?

  1. (i) A parallelogram with a right angle is a rectangle.
  2. (ii) A rhombus with perpendicular diagonals is a square.
  3. (iii) If the diagonals of a parallelogram are equal, then it is a rectangle.
  • (i) True. In a parallelogram, opposite angles are equal and adjacent angles add up to 180°. So if one angle is 90°, all four angles become 90°, making it a rectangle.
  • (ii) False. Every rhombus has perpendicular diagonals, even when its angles are not 90°. It is a square only when its diagonals are also equal (or one of its angles is 90°).
  • (iii) True. If the diagonals of a parallelogram are equal, we can prove by SSS congruence (ΔABC ≅ ΔBAD) that adjacent angles are equal and add up to 180°, so each angle is 90°.
Q2

The diagonal AC of a parallelogram ABCD bisects ∠A. Show that it also bisects ∠C and that ABCD is a rhombus.

Proof:
1. Since ABCD is a parallelogram, AB || DC and AD || BC.
2. Since AC bisects ∠A, we have ∠DAC = ∠BAC.
3. Because AB || DC, alternate interior angles are equal: ∠BAC = ∠DCA.
4. Similarly, because AD || BC, alternate interior angles are equal: ∠DAC = ∠BCA.
5. From (2), (3), and (4), we get ∠DCA = ∠BCA. This shows that AC also bisects ∠C.
6. Also, in ΔADC, since ∠DAC = ∠DCA, the sides opposite to equal angles are equal, so AD = DC.
7. Since opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal and adjacent sides AD and DC are also equal, all four sides are equal (AB = BC = CD = DA). Hence, ABCD is a rhombus.

Diagonal AC bisects ∠A and ∠C ⇒ AD = DC ⇒ ABCD is a Rhombus
Q3

The following questions examine converses of true properties. Answer them with Yes or No. If your answer is No, what extra condition can you add so that the answer becomes Yes?

  • (i) If the diagonals of a quadrilateral ABCD bisect its angles, must ABCD be a rhombus?
  • (ii) If the diagonals of a quadrilateral ABCD bisect each other at right angles, must ABCD be a rhombus?
  • (iii) If the diagonals of a quadrilateral ABCD are of equal length, must ABCD be a rectangle?
  • (i) Yes. Since AC bisects ∠A and ∠C, we get ΔABC ≅ ΔADC by ASA, so AB = AD and CB = CD. Similarly, BD bisecting ∠B and ∠D gives AB = BC and AD = CD. Thus all four sides are equal, so ABCD is a rhombus.
  • (ii) Yes. Since the diagonals bisect each other, ABCD is a parallelogram; since they intersect at right angles, the four small triangles formed by the diagonals are congruent by SAS, making all four sides equal.
  • (iii) No. An isosceles trapezium also has diagonals of equal length, but it is not a rectangle. Extra condition: Add that the diagonals also bisect each other (or that ABCD is a parallelogram).
Q4

Let ABCD be a parallelogram with AB ≠ BC. Show that the pairwise intersection points of the four angle bisectors form the vertices of a rectangle. Why did we assume AB ≠ BC?

Answer:
Let the bisectors of adjacent angles meet to form the inner quadrilateral PQRS (say, bisectors of ∠A and ∠D meet at S, ∠A and ∠B at P, ∠B and ∠C at Q, and ∠C and ∠D at R).
1. In parallelogram ABCD, adjacent angles are supplementary, so ∠A + ∠D = 180°.
2. In ΔASD formed by the bisectors of ∠A and ∠D, ∠DAS + ∠ADS = ½∠A + ½∠D = ½(180°) = 90°.
3. By the angle sum property in ΔASD, ∠ASD = 180° − 90° = 90° (and its vertically opposite interior angle in PQRS is also 90°).
4. In the exact same way, the angles at P, Q, and R are also 90°. Since all four angles of PQRS are 90°, PQRS is a rectangle.
Why we assumed AB ≠ BC: If AB = BC, ABCD would be a rhombus. In a rhombus, the diagonals themselves bisect the vertex angles, so all four angle bisectors would meet at a single point (the centre) instead of enclosing a rectangle.

Pairwise intersections of the four angle bisectors of a parallelogram form rectangle PQRS
Q1

(i) If P, Q, R are the midpoints of sides AB, AC, BC respectively of ΔABC, show that ΔPQR is congruent to ΔQPA and to two other triangles which you should identify.
(ii) Suppose someone erases ΔABC, leaving only ΔPQR on the paper. Can you reconstruct ΔABC from ΔPQR?

  • (i) By the Midpoint Theorem, PQ || BC, QR || AB, and PR || AC. Since PQ || RC and QR || AP, APRQ is a parallelogram, so its diagonal PQ divides it into two congruent triangles: ΔPQR ≅ ΔQPA. Similarly, in parallelograms PBQR and PQCR, we get ΔPQR ≅ ΔRBP and ΔPQR ≅ ΔCQR.
  • (ii) Yes, we can reconstruct ΔABC. Through P, draw a line parallel to QR; through Q, draw a line parallel to PR; and through R, draw a line parallel to PQ. The three points where these lines intersect are the original vertices A, B, and C.
Medial triangle ΔPQR divides ΔABC into 4 congruent triangles: ΔPQR ≅ ΔQPA ≅ ΔRBP ≅ ΔCQR
Q2

In ΔABC, let M and N be midpoints of AB and AC respectively. Let D be any point on BC. Show that MN bisects AD.

Proof:
1. In ΔABC, M and N are the midpoints of AB and AC, so by the Midpoint Theorem, MN || BC.
2. Let AD meet MN at point X.
3. Now look at ΔABD: M is the midpoint of side AB, and MX || BD (since MN || BC).
4. By the converse of the Midpoint Theorem, the line through the midpoint M parallel to BD must bisect the third side AD.
5. Therefore, X is the midpoint of AD, which means MN bisects AD.

Midsegment MN ∥ BC bisects segment AD at X (AX = XD)
Q4

Suppose the midpoints of sides AB, BC, CD and DA of a quadrilateral ABCD are P, Q, R and S respectively.
(i) Show that PR and QS bisect each other.
(ii) Show that if AC = BD, then PR and QS are perpendicular. Is the converse true?

  • (i) By the Midpoint Theorem applied to ΔABC and ΔADC, PQ || AC and PQ = ½AC, while SR || AC and SR = ½AC. Since PQ || SR and PQ = SR, PQRS is a parallelogram. Because the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, PR and QS bisect each other.
  • (ii) Similarly, PS = QR = ½BD. If AC = BD, then all four sides of parallelogram PQRS are equal (PQ = QR = RS = SP = ½AC), so PQRS is a rhombus. Since the diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular, PR ⊥ QS. Yes, the converse is also true: if PR ⊥ QS, the parallelogram PQRS is a rhombus, so PQ = PS, which gives ½AC = ½BD, or AC = BD.
Varignon Parallelogram PQRS: Diagonals PR and QS bisect each other
Q1

Justify why the plane cannot be tiled with a regular pentagon.

Answer: Tiling the plane requires shapes to fit together around a vertex such that the sum of the angles meeting at that vertex equals exactly 360°, leaving no gaps or overlaps.
The interior angle of a regular pentagon is 108°.
If we try to fit regular pentagons around a vertex, three pentagons give 108° × 3 = 324°. This leaves a gap of 36°. A fourth pentagon would make the sum 432°, causing an overlap. Because 360 is not evenly divisible by 108, the plane cannot be tiled with regular pentagons.

Three Regular Pentagons at a Vertex Leave a 36° Uncovered Gap (4 × 108° = 432° > 360° Overlaps)
Q1

Using a fact about parallelograms, show how to tile the plane using any given triangle.

Answer:
1. Take any triangle and make an exact copy of it.
2. Rotate the copy by 180° and place it against the original triangle along a matching side.
3. Because the alternate interior angles along that shared side are equal, the opposite outer sides become parallel and equal, forming a parallelogram.
4. Since we already know that copies of any parallelogram can tile the entire plane side-by-side in parallel strips, tiling the plane with these parallelograms automatically tiles it with the given triangle.

Q3

You know that the sum of angles of a quadrilateral is 360°, even for a non-convex quadrilateral. Now consider a self-intersecting quadrilateral ABCD, where AB and CD intersect at point E. Show that ∠A + ∠B + ∠C + ∠D < 360°.

Proof:
1. Since sides AB and CD intersect at E, the figure consists of two triangles, ΔADE and ΔBCE.
2. By the angle sum property of a triangle:
In ΔADE: ∠A + ∠D + ∠AED = 180°
In ΔBCE: ∠B + ∠C + ∠BEC = 180°
3. Adding these two equations gives:
∠A + ∠B + ∠C + ∠D + ∠AED + ∠BEC = 360°
4. Therefore, ∠A + ∠B + ∠C + ∠D = 360° − (∠AED + ∠BEC).
5. Since ∠AED and ∠BEC are positive angles of the two triangles (> 0°), subtracting their sum from 360° leaves less than 360°. Hence, ∠A + ∠B + ∠C + ∠D < 360°.

Self-intersecting quadrilateral ABCD forming ΔADE and ΔBCE: ∠A + ∠B + ∠C + ∠D < 360°
Q4

In a parallelogram ABCD, two points P and Q are taken on diagonal BD such that DP = BQ. Show that APCQ is a parallelogram.

Proof:
1. Let the diagonals AC and BD of parallelogram ABCD intersect at O.
2. Since the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, OA = OC and OD = OB.
3. We are given that DP = BQ.
4. Subtracting these equal lengths from OD and OB gives:
OP = OD − DP and OQ = OB − BQ, so OP = OQ.
5. Now in quadrilateral APCQ, its diagonals AC and PQ intersect at O such that OA = OC and OP = OQ.
6. Since the diagonals of APCQ bisect each other, APCQ is a parallelogram.

Q8

The diagonals AC and BD of a parallelogram ABCD intersect at O. A line through O meets AB and CD at points P and Q respectively. Show that O is the midpoint of PQ.

Proof:
1. Since ABCD is a parallelogram, AB || DC and its diagonals bisect each other at O, so OA = OC.
2. In ΔAPO and ΔCQO:
- ∠PAO = ∠QCO (alternate interior angles, since AB || DC and AC is a transversal)
- OA = OC (diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other)
- ∠AOP = ∠COQ (vertically opposite angles)
3. By the ASA congruence rule, ΔAPO ≅ ΔCQO.
4. Therefore, by CPCT, OP = OQ, which means O is the midpoint of PQ.

ΔAPO ≅ ΔCQO (by ASA) ⇒ OP = OQ
Q12

Suppose ABCD is a parallelogram and M, N are midpoints of AB and CD respectively. Show that segments DM and BN trisect segment AC.

Proof:
1. ABCD is a parallelogram, so AB || DC and AB = DC.
2. M and N are midpoints, so AM = MB = 1/2 AB, and DN = NC = 1/2 DC.
3. Because AB = DC, it implies AM = NC and MB = DN. Also, MB || DN.
4. A quadrilateral with one pair of equal and parallel opposite sides is a parallelogram (Theorem 5). Thus, MBND is a parallelogram, which means DM || BN.
5. Let DM intersect AC at point P, and BN intersect AC at point Q.
6. In ΔABQ, M is the midpoint of AB, and MP || BQ (since DM || BN). By the Converse of the Midpoint Theorem, P must be the midpoint of AQ. Therefore, AP = PQ.
7. In ΔCDP, N is the midpoint of CD, and NQ || DP (since BN || DM). By the Converse of the Midpoint Theorem, Q must be the midpoint of PC. Therefore, PQ = QC.
8. Combining these gives AP = PQ = QC. The points P and Q divide the diagonal AC into three equal parts, proving that DM and BN trisect AC.

Parallel segments DM and BN trisect diagonal AC at P and Q (AP = PQ = QC)
Q17

Show that the sum of angles of a non-planar quadrilateral is always less than 360°.

Answer:
Imagine a flat quadrilateral ABCD folded along its diagonal AC like a hinge so that it is no longer in one plane.
1. When ABCD lies flat on a plane, the sum of its four interior angles is 360°.
2. As we fold the two triangles ΔABC and ΔADC along AC, the vertices B and D move closer to each other in 3D space, while the angles ∠B (in ΔABC) and ∠D (in ΔADC) do not change.
3. In ΔABD, the sides AB and AD stay the same length, but the third side BD becomes shorter than it was when flat. Because a shorter opposite side corresponds to a smaller angle in a triangle, ∠BAD becomes smaller than the sum of ∠BAC and ∠CAD.
4. By the same reasoning in ΔCBD, shortening BD makes ∠BCD smaller than it was when flat.
5. Since ∠A and ∠C both decrease while ∠B and ∠D stay the same, the sum of the four angles in the non-planar quadrilateral is strictly less than 360°.

Q21

Sum of angles of a polygon. What is the sum of angles of a (planar non-self-intersecting) n-gon? Find a formula in terms of n and prove it.

Answer:
1. The sum of the interior angles of a planar $n$-gon is $(n - 2) \times 180^\circ$.
2. Proof: Pick any vertex of the $n$-gon and join it to all the other non-adjacent vertices by drawing diagonals.
3. Since we cannot draw a diagonal to the chosen vertex itself or to its 2 adjacent neighbours, we can draw $(n - 3)$ diagonals from that vertex.
4. These $(n - 3)$ diagonals split the $n$-gon into $(n - 2)$ non-overlapping triangles.
5. Because the angles of each triangle add up to $180^\circ$, and all their angles together make up the interior angles of the $n$-gon, the sum of the interior angles is $(n - 2) \times 180^\circ$.
