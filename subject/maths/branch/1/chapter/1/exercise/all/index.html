Q1

Exercise Set 1.1

Referring to Fig. 1.3, answer the following questions:

  1. If D₁R₁ represents the door to Reiaan's room, how far is the door from the left wall (the y-axis) of the room? How far is the door from the x-axis?
  2. What are the coordinates of D₁?
  3. If R₁ is the point (11.5, 0), how wide is the door? Do you think this is a comfortable width for the room door? If a person in a wheelchair wants to enter the room, will he/she be able to do so easily?
  4. If B₁(0, 1.5) and B₂(0, 4) represent the ends of the bathroom door, is the bathroom door narrower or wider than the room door?
Distance along an axis is the absolute difference between coordinates: $|x_2 - x_1|$ or $|y_2 - y_1|$.
  1. The door starts at point D₁, which is 8 feet away from the left wall (the y-axis). Since the door lies right on the x-axis, its distance from the x-axis is 0 feet.
  2. The coordinates of D₁ are $(8, 0)$.
  3. Width of the door $= 11.5 - 8 = 3.5$ feet. Yes, 3.5 feet is a comfortable width for a bedroom door. A standard wheelchair needs at least 32 inches (about 2.7 feet) of space to pass through, so a person in a wheelchair can easily enter the room.
  4. Width of the bathroom door $= 4 - 1.5 = 2.5$ feet. Since 2.5 feet is less than 3.5 feet, the bathroom door is narrower than the room door.

  2. Are the doors in your school suitable for people in wheelchairs?
Consider architectural standards for accessibility: standard interior doors are around 32 to 36 inches.

  1. Most normal room doors at home are about 3 feet (36 inches) wide, while bathroom doors are usually around 2.5 feet (30 inches) wide. Classroom doors in schools are often 3.5 to 4 feet wide.
  2. Yes, classroom doors that are at least 3 feet wide are wide enough for a wheelchair to pass through comfortably (since a wheelchair needs at least 32 inches of clear width).
Q1

Think and Reflect

  1. What is the x-coordinate of a point on the y-axis?
  2. Is there a similar generalisation for a point on the x-axis?
  3. Does point Q(y, x) ever coincide with point P(x, y)? Justify your answer.
  4. If $x \ne y$ then $(x, y) \ne (y, x)$ and $(x, y) = (y, x)$ if and only if $x = y$. Is this claim true?
Every point on the y-axis has $x = 0$, and every point on the x-axis has $y = 0$. An ordered pair $(x, y) = (y, x)$ if and only if $x = y$.
  1. The x-coordinate of any point on the y-axis is always $0$.
  2. Yes, the y-coordinate of any point on the x-axis is always $0$.
  3. Point $Q(y, x)$ coincides with point $P(x, y)$ only when $x = y$. For example, if $x = 4$ and $y = 4$, both points become $(4, 4)$ and represent the exact same point on the graph.
  4. Yes, this statement is true. The position of a point depends on the order of its coordinates. If $x \ne y$, swapping the numbers gives a different point on the plane — for example, $(2, 5)$ is a different point from $(5, 2)$.

2. If the bathroom door has a hinge at B₁ and opens into the bedroom, will it hit the wardrobe? Are there any changes you would suggest if the door is made wider?

Calculate the swing radius of the door ($r = 4 - 1.5 = 2.5$) and compare with the distance to the wardrobe at $x = 3$.

The hinge is at $B_1(0, 1.5)$ and the other end of the door is at $B_2(0, 4)$, so the door is $4 - 1.5 = 2.5$ feet wide. When it swings open into the bedroom, it reaches out to a distance of $2.5$ feet from the left wall (up to $x = 2.5$). If the door is made wider (say, $3.5$ feet), it will swing out to $x = 3.5$ and hit the wardrobe. To fix this, we could either shift the wardrobe further to the right along the bottom wall, or make the bathroom door open inward into the bathroom instead.

4. Other rooms in the house:

  1. Reiaan's room door leads from the dining room which has the length 18 ft and width 15 ft. The length of the dining room extends from point P to point A. Sketch the dining room and mark the coordinates of its corners.
  2. Place a rectangular 5 ft × 3 ft dining table precisely in the centre of the dining room. Write down the coordinates of the feet of the table.
Midpoint formula: $\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$. Add and subtract half-lengths to determine corner coordinates.
  1. The dining room runs along the x-axis from $P(-6, 0)$ to $A(12, 0)$, which gives a length of $12 - (-6) = 18$ feet. Since it shares the wall $PA$ on the x-axis and has a width of 15 feet, it extends 15 units down along the negative y-axis. So the four corners of the dining room are $P(-6, 0)$, $A(12, 0)$, $(12, -15)$, and $(-6, -15)$.
  2. Step 1: Find the centre of the dining room.
    Midpoint of x-coordinates $= \frac{-6 + 12}{2} = 3$
    Midpoint of y-coordinates $= \frac{0 + (-15)}{2} = -7.5$
    So the centre of the room is $(3, -7.5)$.
    Step 2: Find the corners of the $5\text{ ft} \times 3\text{ ft}$ table.
    From the centre $(3, -7.5)$, the table extends $2.5$ ft left and right ($3 \pm 2.5$) and $1.5$ ft up and down ($-7.5 \pm 1.5$).
    Therefore, the four feet of the table are at $(0.5, -6)$, $(5.5, -6)$, $(5.5, -9)$, and $(0.5, -9)$.
Q1

Think and Reflect (Page 9)

  1. In moving from A(3, 4) to D(7, 1), what distance has been covered along the x-axis? What about the distance along the y-axis?
  2. Can these distances help you find the distance AD?
Baudhāyana-Pythagoras theorem: $d = \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2}$.
  1. Distance covered along the x-axis $= 7 - 3 = 4$ units.
    Distance covered along the y-axis $= 4 - 1 = 3$ units.
  2. Yes! The horizontal and vertical distances form the two perpendicular sides of a right-angled triangle, with $AD$ as the hypotenuse. By the Baudhāyana-Pythagoras theorem:
    $AD = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} = 5\text{ units}$.
Q2

Think and Reflect (Page 11)

  1. What has remained the same and what has changed with this reflection across the y-axis?
  2. Would these observations be the same if $\Delta ADM$ is reflected in the x-axis (instead of the y-axis)?
Reflection across y-axis transforms $(x, y) \to (-x, y)$. Reflection across x-axis transforms $(x, y) \to (x, -y)$.
  1. What stayed the same: The side lengths ($AD$, $DM$, and $MA$), shape, and area of the triangle, as well as the y-coordinates of all three vertices.
    What changed: The x-coordinates of all the vertices changed sign (from positive to negative).
  2. If $\Delta ADM$ is reflected across the x-axis instead, the shape, size, and side lengths of the triangle will still remain the same. However, this time the x-coordinates will stay the same and the y-coordinates will change sign.
Q1

End-of-Chapter Exercises

1. What are the x-coordinate and y-coordinate of the point of intersection of the two axes?

The origin is the reference starting point where $x = 0$ and $y = 0$.

The point where the x-axis and y-axis intersect is called the origin. Both its x-coordinate and y-coordinate are $0$, so it is written as $(0, 0)$.

Q2

End-of-Chapter Exercises

2. Point W has x-coordinate equal to 5. Can you predict the coordinates of point H which is on the line through W parallel to the y-axis? Which quadrants can H lie in?

A line parallel to the y-axis has the equation $x = c$. Points with positive x-coordinates lie in Quadrants I and IV.

Since the line through $W$ is parallel to the y-axis, every point on this line has the same x-coordinate as $W$, which is $5$. So the coordinates of $H$ are of the form $(5, y)$, where $y$ can be any real number.

Since the x-coordinate is positive ($+5$), point $H$ can lie in Quadrant I (when $y > 0$) or Quadrant IV (when $y < 0$), or on the positive x-axis (when $y = 0$).

Q3

End-of-Chapter Exercises

3. Consider the points R(3, 0), A(0, -2), M(-5, -2) and P(-5, 2). If they are joined in the same order, predict:
(i) Two sides of RAMP that are perpendicular to each other.
(ii) One side of RAMP that is parallel to one of the axes.
(iii) Two points that are mirror images of each other in one axis. Which axis will this be?

Horizontal lines have constant y; vertical lines have constant x. Mirror reflection across x-axis inverts the sign of y.
  1. Side $AM$ joins $A(0, -2)$ and $M(-5, -2)$, which have the same y-coordinate ($-2$), so $AM$ is a horizontal line. Side $MP$ joins $M(-5, -2)$ and $P(-5, 2)$, which have the same x-coordinate ($-5$), so $MP$ is a vertical line. Therefore, sides $AM$ and $MP$ are perpendicular to each other.
  2. Side $AM$ is parallel to the x-axis (since $y = -2$ for both points), and side $MP$ is parallel to the y-axis (since $x = -5$ for both points).
  3. Points $M(-5, -2)$ and $P(-5, 2)$ have the same x-coordinate ($-5$) and opposite y-coordinates ($-2$ and $2$). So they are mirror images of each other across the x-axis.

End-of-Chapter Exercises

4. Plot point Z(5, -6) on the Cartesian plane. Construct a right-angled triangle IZN and find the lengths of the three sides.

Distance between $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$: $\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$.

Plot $Z(5, -6)$ in Quadrant IV. To make a right-angled triangle $\Delta IZN$, we can take $I(5, 0)$ on the x-axis directly above $Z$, and $N(0, 0)$ at the origin, so that $\angle ZIN = 90^\circ$.

  • Length of vertical side $IZ = |0 - (-6)| = 6$ units.
  • Length of horizontal side $IN = |5 - 0| = 5$ units.
  • By Pythagoras' theorem, the hypotenuse $ZN = \sqrt{5^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{25 + 36} = \sqrt{61}\text{ units}$ (about $7.81$ units).
Q5

End-of-Chapter Exercises

5. What would a system of coordinates be like if we did not have negative numbers? Would this system allow us to locate all the points on a 2-D plane?

Without negative numbers, we only have $[0, \infty) \times [0, \infty)$, which represents Quadrant I.

Without negative numbers, we could only plot points where both $x$ and $y$ are zero or positive (that is, Quadrant I and the positive axes). So no, this system would not allow us to locate all points on a 2-D plane, because we wouldn't be able to mark any point to the left of the y-axis or below the x-axis.

Q6

End-of-Chapter Exercises

6. Are the points M(-3, -4), A(0, 0) and G(6, 8) on the same straight line? Suggest a method to check this without plotting and joining the points.

Collinearity condition via distance: points A, B, C are collinear if the sum of two smaller distances equals the largest distance ($AB + BC = AC$).

Method: Without plotting the points, we can find the distances $MA$, $AG$, and $MG$ using the distance formula. If the sum of the two shorter distances equals the longest distance, the three points lie on the same straight line (collinear).

  • $MA = \sqrt{(0 - (-3))^2 + (0 - (-4))^2} = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$
  • $AG = \sqrt{(6 - 0)^2 + (8 - 0)^2} = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10$
  • $MG = \sqrt{(6 - (-3))^2 + (8 - (-4))^2} = \sqrt{9^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{81 + 144} = \sqrt{225} = 15$

Since $MA + AG = 5 + 10 = 15 = MG$, the points $M$, $A$, and $G$ lie on the same straight line.

Q7

End-of-Chapter Exercises

7. Use your method (from Problem 6) to check if the points R(-5, -1), B(-2, -5) and C(4, -12) are on the same straight line.

Compute $RB$, $BC$, and $RC$. If $RB + BC \ne RC$, the points are not collinear.

Using the distance formula to find the lengths of $RB$, $BC$, and $RC$:

  • $RB = \sqrt{(-2 - (-5))^2 + (-5 - (-1))^2} = \sqrt{3^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$
  • $BC = \sqrt{(4 - (-2))^2 + (-12 - (-5))^2} = \sqrt{6^2 + (-7)^2} = \sqrt{36 + 49} = \sqrt{85} \approx 9.22$
  • $RC = \sqrt{(4 - (-5))^2 + (-12 - (-1))^2} = \sqrt{9^2 + (-11)^2} = \sqrt{81 + 121} = \sqrt{202} \approx 14.21$

Here, $RB + BC = 5 + \sqrt{85} \approx 14.22$, which is not equal to $RC$ ($\sqrt{202} \approx 14.21$). Since $RB + BC \ne RC$, the points $R$, $B$, and $C$ are not on the same straight line.

Q8

End-of-Chapter Exercises

8. Using the origin as one vertex, plot the vertices of:
(i) A right-angled isosceles triangle.
(ii) An isosceles triangle with one vertex in Quadrant III and the other in Quadrant IV.

An isosceles right triangle has legs of equal length meeting at $90^\circ$. Symmetry across the y-axis ensures equal distance from origin.
  1. Take the origin $O(0, 0)$ as one vertex, and mark $A(5, 0)$ on the x-axis and $B(0, 5)$ on the y-axis. Then $OA = OB = 5$ units and $\angle AOB = 90^\circ$, so $\Delta OAB$ is a right-angled isosceles triangle.
  2. Take $O(0, 0)$ as the first vertex, and choose two points symmetric across the negative y-axis — for example, $P(-4, -5)$ in Quadrant III and $Q(4, -5)$ in Quadrant IV.
    Then $OP = \sqrt{(-4)^2 + (-5)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 25} = \sqrt{41}$ and $OQ = \sqrt{4^2 + (-5)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 25} = \sqrt{41}$.
    Since $OP = OQ$, $\Delta OPQ$ is an isosceles triangle.

9. The following table shows the coordinates of points S, M and T. In each case, state whether M is the midpoint of segment ST. Justify your answer.
(a) S(-3, 0), M(0, 0), T(3, 0)
(b) S(2, 3), M(3, 4), T(4, 5)
(c) S(0, 0), M(0, 5), T(0, -10)
(d) S(-8, 7), M(0, -2), T(6, -3)

Midpoint formula: $M = \left(\frac{x_S + x_T}{2}, \frac{y_S + y_T}{2}\right)$.
  1. Yes. Midpoint of $ST = \left(\frac{-3+3}{2}, \frac{0+0}{2}\right) = (0, 0)$, which is $M$.
  2. Yes. Midpoint of $ST = \left(\frac{2+4}{2}, \frac{3+5}{2}\right) = (3, 4)$, which is $M$.
  3. No. Midpoint of $ST = \left(\frac{0+0}{2}, \frac{0+(-10)}{2}\right) = (0, -5)$, whereas $M$ is $(0, 5)$.
  4. No. Midpoint of $ST = \left(\frac{-8+6}{2}, \frac{7+(-3)}{2}\right) = (-1, 2)$, which is not equal to $M(0, -2)$.
Q10

End-of-Chapter Exercises

10. When M is the mid-point of ST, can you find any connection between the coordinates of M, S and T? Use the connection you found to find the coordinates of B given that M(-7, 1) is the midpoint of A(3, -4) and B(x, y).

If M is the midpoint of AB: $x_M = \frac{x_A + x_B}{2} \implies x_B = 2x_M - x_A$.

Connection: When $M$ is the midpoint of $ST$, the coordinates of $M$ are the averages of the x- and y-coordinates of $S(x_1, y_1)$ and $T(x_2, y_2)$:

$M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$

Given $M(-7, 1)$ is the midpoint of $A(3, -4)$ and $B(x, y)$:

  • $\frac{3 + x}{2} = -7 \implies 3 + x = -14 \implies x = -17$
  • $\frac{-4 + y}{2} = 1 \implies -4 + y = 2 \implies y = 6$

Therefore, the coordinates of $B$ are $(-17, 6)$.

Q11

End-of-Chapter Exercises

11. Let P, Q be points of trisection of AB, with P closer to A, and Q closer to B. Using your knowledge of how to find the coordinates of the midpoint of a segment, how would you find the coordinates of P and Q? Do this for the case when the points are A(4, 7) and B(16, -2).

Trisection points divide the segment into three equal parts: P is 1/3 of the way from A to B, and Q is 2/3 of the way.

Since $P$ and $Q$ divide $AB$ into three equal parts ($AP = PQ = QB$), $P$ is the midpoint of $AQ$ and $Q$ is the midpoint of $PB$. Let $P = (x_p, y_p)$ and $Q = (x_q, y_q)$.

1. Finding the x-coordinates:

  • $x_p = \frac{4 + x_q}{2} \implies x_q = 2x_p - 4$
  • $x_q = \frac{x_p + 16}{2} \implies 2x_q - x_p = 16$

Substituting $x_q = 2x_p - 4$ into the second equation:
$2(2x_p - 4) - x_p = 16 \implies 3x_p - 8 = 16 \implies 3x_p = 24 \implies x_p = 8$.
Then $x_q = 2(8) - 4 = 12$.

2. Finding the y-coordinates:

  • $y_p = \frac{7 + y_q}{2} \implies y_q = 2y_p - 7$
  • $y_q = \frac{y_p + (-2)}{2} \implies 2y_q - y_p = -2$

Substituting $y_q = 2y_p - 7$ into the second equation:
$2(2y_p - 7) - y_p = -2 \implies 3y_p - 14 = -2 \implies 3y_p = 12 \implies y_p = 4$.
Then $y_q = 2(4) - 7 = 1$.

Therefore, the points of trisection are $P(8, 4)$ and $Q(12, 1)$.

Q12

End-of-Chapter Exercises

12. (i) Given the points A(1, -8), B(-4, 7) and C(-7, -4), show that they lie on a circle K whose center is the origin O(0, 0). What is the radius of circle K?
(ii) Given the points D(-5, 6) and E(0, 9), check whether D and E lie within the circle, on the circle, or outside the circle K.

Distance from origin $O(0, 0)$ is $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$. Compare this distance to the radius $R$ to determine if a point is inside, on, or outside the circle.
  1. Let us find the distance of each point from the centre $O(0, 0)$:
    • $OA = \sqrt{1^2 + (-8)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 64} = \sqrt{65}$
    • $OB = \sqrt{(-4)^2 + 7^2} = \sqrt{16 + 49} = \sqrt{65}$
    • $OC = \sqrt{(-7)^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{49 + 16} = \sqrt{65}$
    Since $OA = OB = OC = \sqrt{65}$, all three points are at the same distance from the origin, so they lie on circle $K$. The radius of circle $K$ is $\sqrt{65}$ units (about $8.06$ units).
  2. Now let us find the distances of $D$ and $E$ from $O(0, 0)$:
    • $OD = \sqrt{(-5)^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{25 + 36} = \sqrt{61}$. Since $\sqrt{61} < \sqrt{65}$, point $D$ lies inside the circle.
    • $OE = \sqrt{0^2 + 9^2} = \sqrt{81} = 9$. Since $\sqrt{81} > \sqrt{65}$, point $E$ lies outside the circle.
Q13

End-of-Chapter Exercises

13. The midpoints of the sides of triangle ABC are the points D, E, and F. Given that the coordinates of D, E, and F are (5, 1), (6, 5), and (0, 3), respectively, find the coordinates of A, B and C.

Use midpoint equations: $x_1+x_2=2x_D$, $x_2+x_3=2x_E$, $x_3+x_1=2x_F$. Sum all three equations to find $x_1+x_2+x_3$.

Let the vertices of $\Delta ABC$ be $A(x_1, y_1)$, $B(x_2, y_2)$, and $C(x_3, y_3)$. Using the midpoint formula:

  • Since $D(5, 1)$ is the midpoint of $AB$: $x_1 + x_2 = 10$ and $y_1 + y_2 = 2$
  • Since $E(6, 5)$ is the midpoint of $BC$: $x_2 + x_3 = 12$ and $y_2 + y_3 = 10$
  • Since $F(0, 3)$ is the midpoint of $CA$: $x_3 + x_1 = 0$ and $y_3 + y_1 = 6$

Step 1: Find the x-coordinates.
Adding the three x-equations: $2(x_1 + x_2 + x_3) = 10 + 12 + 0 = 22 \implies x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 11$.

  • $x_1 = 11 - (x_2 + x_3) = 11 - 12 = -1$
  • $x_2 = 11 - (x_3 + x_1) = 11 - 0 = 11$
  • $x_3 = 11 - (x_1 + x_2) = 11 - 10 = 1$

Step 2: Find the y-coordinates.
Adding the three y-equations: $2(y_1 + y_2 + y_3) = 2 + 10 + 6 = 18 \implies y_1 + y_2 + y_3 = 9$.

  • $y_1 = 9 - (y_2 + y_3) = 9 - 10 = -1$
  • $y_2 = 9 - (y_3 + y_1) = 9 - 6 = 3$
  • $y_3 = 9 - (y_1 + y_2) = 9 - 2 = 7$

Therefore, the vertices are $A(-1, -1)$, $B(11, 3)$, and $C(1, 7)$.

14. A city has two main roads which cross each other at the centre of the city. These two roads are along the North-South (N-S) direction and East-West (E-W) direction. All the other streets of the city run parallel to these roads and are 200 m apart. There are 10 streets in each direction.
(i) Using 1 cm = 200 m, draw a model of the city in your notebook. Represent the roads/streets by single lines.
(ii) ...find: (a) how many street intersections can be referred to as (4, 3). (b) how many street intersections can be referred to as (3, 4).

Every ordered pair $(x, y)$ in the Cartesian plane defines a uniquely identifiable intersection.
  1. (Notebook activity: Draw two perpendicular axes for the main E-W and N-S roads, and draw 10 parallel lines in each direction spaced $1\text{ cm}$ apart.)
  2. (a) Only one street intersection can be referred to as $(4, 3)$, where the 4th North-South street and the 3rd East-West street cross.
    (b) Similarly, only one street intersection can be referred to as $(3, 4)$, where the 3rd North-South street and the 4th East-West street cross.
Q15

End-of-Chapter Exercises

15. A computer graphics program displays images on a rectangular screen whose coordinate system has the origin at the bottom-left corner. The screen is 800 pixels wide and 600 pixels high. A circular icon of radius 80 pixels is drawn with its centre at the point A(100, 150). Another circular icon of radius 100 pixels is drawn with its centre at the point B(250, 230). Determine:
(i) whether any part of either circle lies outside the screen.
(ii) whether the two circles intersect each other.

Circles intersect if the distance between their centers is less than or equal to the sum of their radii ($d \le r_1 + r_2$) and greater than or equal to $|r_1 - r_2|$.
  1. The screen goes from $x = 0$ to $800$ and $y = 0$ to $600$.
    • Circle A (centre $(100, 150)$, radius $80$):
      x-coordinates go from $100 - 80 = 20$ to $100 + 80 = 180$.
      y-coordinates go from $150 - 80 = 70$ to $150 + 80 = 230$.
    • Circle B (centre $(250, 230)$, radius $100$):
      x-coordinates go from $250 - 100 = 150$ to $250 + 100 = 350$.
      y-coordinates go from $230 - 100 = 130$ to $230 + 100 = 330$.
    Since all these coordinates stay between $0$ and $800$ for $x$ and between $0$ and $600$ for $y$, neither circle goes outside the screen.
  2. Distance between the centres $A(100, 150)$ and $B(250, 230)$:
    $AB = \sqrt{(250 - 100)^2 + (230 - 150)^2} = \sqrt{150^2 + 80^2} = \sqrt{22500 + 6400} = \sqrt{28900} = 170\text{ pixels}$.
    Sum of the radii $= 80 + 100 = 180\text{ pixels}$, and difference of the radii $= 100 - 80 = 20\text{ pixels}$.
    Since the distance between the centres ($170$) is less than the sum of the radii ($180$) and greater than their difference ($20$), yes, the two circles intersect each other.
Q16

End-of-Chapter Exercises

16. Plot the points A(2, 1), B(-1, 2), C(-2, -1), and D(1, -2) in the coordinate plane. Is ABCD a square? Can you explain why? What is the area of this square?

A quadrilateral is a square if all four sides are equal and both diagonals are equal ($d_1 = d_2 = s\sqrt{2}$).

Yes, $ABCD$ is a square. We can show this by finding the lengths of all four sides and both diagonals using the distance formula:

1. Lengths of the sides:

  • $AB = \sqrt{(-1 - 2)^2 + (2 - 1)^2} = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{9 + 1} = \sqrt{10}$
  • $BC = \sqrt{(-2 - (-1))^2 + (-1 - 2)^2} = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + (-3)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 9} = \sqrt{10}$
  • $CD = \sqrt{(1 - (-2))^2 + (-2 - (-1))^2} = \sqrt{3^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 1} = \sqrt{10}$
  • $DA = \sqrt{(2 - 1)^2 + (1 - (-2))^2} = \sqrt{1^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{1 + 9} = \sqrt{10}$

2. Lengths of the diagonals:

  • $AC = \sqrt{(-2 - 2)^2 + (-1 - 1)^2} = \sqrt{(-4)^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 4} = \sqrt{20}$
  • $BD = \sqrt{(1 - (-1))^2 + (-2 - 2)^2} = \sqrt{2^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 16} = \sqrt{20}$

Since all four sides are equal ($\sqrt{10}$ units) and both diagonals are equal ($\sqrt{20}$ units), $ABCD$ is a square.

3. Area of the square:

$\text{Area} = (\text{side})^2 = (\sqrt{10})^2 = 10\text{ sq. units}$

Master Formula & SI Unit Hub

Every essential Class 9 Mathematics (Ganita Prakash) and Science (Physics & Chemistry) formula, algebraic identity, mensuration table, and SI unit in one place.

Maths • 01
Polynomials & All 10 Standard Algebraic Identities
  • Identity I (Square of a Binomial Sum): \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\)
  • Identity II (Square of a Binomial Difference): \((a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2\)
  • Identity III (Difference of Squares): \(a^2 - b^2 = (a + b)(a - b)\)
  • Identity IV (Product with Common Term): \((x + a)(x + b) = x^2 + (a + b)x + ab\)
  • Identity V (Square of a Trinomial): \((a + b + c)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca\)
  • Identity VI (Cube of a Sum): \((a + b)^3 = a^3 + b^3 + 3ab(a + b) = a^3 + 3a^2b + 3ab^2 + b^3\)
  • Identity VII (Cube of a Difference): \((a - b)^3 = a^3 - b^3 - 3ab(a - b) = a^3 - 3a^2b + 3ab^2 - b^3\)
  • Identity VIII (Sum of Cubes): \(a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)\)
  • Identity IX (Difference of Cubes): \(a^3 - b^3 = (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)\)
  • Identity X (Gauss / Trinomial Cube Identity): \(a^3 + b^3 + c^3 - 3abc = (a + b + c)(a^2 + b^2 + c^2 - ab - bc - ca)\)
  • Special Exam Shortcut: If \(a + b + c = 0\), then \(a^3 + b^3 + c^3 = 3abc\).
  • Remainder Theorem: When a polynomial \(p(x)\) of degree \(\ge 1\) is divided by \((x - a)\), the remainder is \(p(a)\). If \(p(a) = 0\), then \((x - a)\) is a factor of \(p(x)\).

Unit Conversions: \(1\text{ m}^3 = 1000\text{ Litres}\),   \(1\text{ Litre} = 1000\text{ cm}^3\),   \(\pi \approx \frac{22}{7} \approx 3.1416\).

Maths • 03
Coordinate Geometry & Linear Equations in Two Variables
  • Quadrant Sign Rules \((x, y)\): Quadrant I: \((+, +)\) • Quadrant II: \((-, +)\) • Quadrant III: \((-, -)\) • Quadrant IV: \((+, -)\).
  • Points on Axes: Any point on the \(x\)-axis is \((x, 0)\); any point on the \(y\)-axis is \((0, y)\); the Origin is \((0, 0)\).
  • Distance Formula: Distance between \(P(x_1, y_1)\) and \(Q(x_2, y_2)\) is \(PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\).
  • Distance from Origin: Distance of \(P(x, y)\) from \((0, 0)\) is \(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\).
  • Midpoint Formula: The midpoint of segment joining \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\) is \(M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)\).
  • Standard Linear Equation in Two Variables: \(ax + by + c = 0\) (where \(a, b\) are not both zero). Its graph is a straight line with infinitely many solutions.
  • Slope-Intercept Form: \(y = mx + c\), where \(m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\) is the slope (rate of change) and \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept.
Maths • 04
Number Systems, Laws of Exponents & Surds
  • Product & Quotient of Powers: \(a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}\)   and   \(\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}\) (\(a \ne 0\)).
  • Power of a Power & Product: \((a^m)^n = a^{mn}\)   and   \((ab)^m = a^m b^m\).
  • Zero, Negative & Fractional Exponents: \(a^0 = 1\),   \(a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}\),   \(a^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{a^m} = (\sqrt[n]{a})^m\).
  • Laws of Radicals (Surds): \(\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}\),   \(\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}\),   \((\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}) = a - b\).
  • Rationalising the Denominator: Multiply numerator and denominator of \(\frac{1}{a + b\sqrt{x}}\) by its conjugate \((a - b\sqrt{x})\) so the denominator becomes \(a^2 - b^2x\).
Maths • 05
2D Perimeter, Area, Heron's Formula & Polygon Geometry
  • Baudhāyana-Pythagoras Theorem: In a right-angled triangle with legs \(a, b\) and hypotenuse \(c\): \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\).
  • Heron's Formula (Any Triangle with sides \(a, b, c\)): Semi-perimeter \(s = \frac{a + b + c}{2}\);   \(\text{Area} = \sqrt{s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}\).
  • Equilateral Triangle (side \(a\)): \(\text{Area} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2\),   Altitude \(h = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a\),   Perimeter \(= 3a\).
  • Quadrilaterals: Parallelogram Area \(= \text{base} \times \text{height}\);   Rhombus Area \(= \frac{1}{2} \times d_1 \times d_2\);   Trapezium Area \(= \frac{1}{2}(a + b) \times h\).
  • Circle, Sector & Ring: Circumference \(= 2\pi r\),   Area \(= \pi r^2\),   Annulus (Ring) Area \(= \pi(R^2 - r^2)\),   Sector Area \(= \frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\pi r^2\).
  • Polygons (\(n\) sides): Sum of interior angles \(= (n - 2) \times 180^\circ\);   Sum of exterior angles \(= 360^\circ\);   Number of diagonals \(= \frac{n(n - 3)}{2}\).
Maths • 06
Probability, Statistics (Data) & Number Sequences
  • Empirical / Classical Probability: \(P(E) = \frac{\text{Number of outcomes favourable to } E}{\text{Total number of possible outcomes}}\), where \(0 \le P(E) \le 1\).
  • Complementary Event: \(P(\text{not } E) = P(\bar{E}) = 1 - P(E)\).
  • Arithmetic Mean (Average): \(\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}\)   (or for frequency table: \(\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}\)).
  • Median (Sorted Data of size \(n\)): If \(n\) is odd, \(\text{Median} = \left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}}\) term;   if \(n\) is even, \(\text{Median} = \text{Average of } \left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{ and } \left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)^{\text{th}}\) terms.
  • Mode & Range: Mode \(=\) most frequently occurring observation;   \(\text{Range} = \text{Maximum Value} - \text{Minimum Value}\).
  • Linear / Arithmetic Sequence: \(n^{\text{th}}\) term \(T_n = a + (n - 1)d\);   Sum of first \(n\) natural numbers \(= \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}\);   Sum of first \(n\) odd numbers \(= n^2\).
Science • 01
Physics: Describing Motion & Three Equations of Motion
  • Speed & Velocity: \(\text{Speed} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}}\) (Scalar, \(\text{m/s}\));   \(\text{Velocity} = \frac{\text{Displacement}}{\text{Time}}\) (Vector, \(\text{m/s}\)).
  • Conversion Shortcut: \(\text{km/h} \xrightarrow{\times \frac{5}{18}} \text{m/s}\)   and   \(\text{m/s} \xrightarrow{\times \frac{18}{5}} \text{km/h}\).
  • Average Speed & Uniform Acceleration Velocity: \(v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}}\);   for uniform acceleration, \(v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{u + v}{2}\).
  • Acceleration: \(a = \frac{v - u}{t}\)   (SI Unit: \(\text{m/s}^2\); negative acceleration is retardation/deceleration).
  • 1st Equation of Motion (Velocity-Time): \(v = u + at\)
  • 2nd Equation of Motion (Position-Time): \(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2\)
  • 3rd Equation of Motion (Position-Velocity): \(v^2 - u^2 = 2as\)   (or \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\))
  • Uniform Circular Motion Speed: \(v = \frac{2\pi r}{t}\) (speed is constant, velocity changes continuously due to changing direction).
Science • 02
Physics: Force, Laws of Motion, Gravitation & Pressure
  • Linear Momentum: \(p = m \times v\)   (SI Unit: \(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}\)).
  • Newton's Second Law of Motion: \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{t} = \frac{m(v - u)}{t} = m \times a\)   (SI Unit: \(\text{Newton (N)} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2\)).
  • Impulse: \(J = F \times t = m(v - u) = \Delta p\) (Change in momentum).
  • Law of Conservation of Momentum: \(m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2\)   (Recoil velocity of gun: \(V = -\frac{mv}{M}\)).
  • Universal Law of Gravitation: \(F = G\frac{M \cdot m}{d^2}\), where \(G = 6.673 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2\).
  • Acceleration due to Gravity & Weight: \(g = \frac{GM}{R^2} \approx 9.8\text{ m/s}^2\);   \(\text{Weight } W = m \times g\);   \(W_{\text{Moon}} = \frac{1}{6}W_{\text{Earth}}\).
  • Free Fall Equations (downward \(+g\), upward \(-g\)): \(v = u + gt\),   \(h = ut + \frac{1}{2}gt^2\),   \(v^2 = u^2 + 2gh\).
  • Pressure, Density & Buoyancy: \(P = \frac{\text{Thrust (F)}}{\text{Area (A)}}\) (\(\text{Pa} = \text{N/m}^2\));   \(\text{Density } \rho = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}\) (\(\text{kg/m}^3\));   \(\text{Relative Density} = \frac{\rho_{\text{substance}}}{\rho_{\text{water}}}\) (No unit).
Science • 03
Physics: Work, Energy, Power & Simple Machines
  • Work Done by a Constant Force: \(W = F \times s\) (when force is along displacement; \(W = 0\) if \(\theta = 90^\circ\) or \(s = 0\)). SI Unit: \(\text{Joule (J)} = 1\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\).
  • Kinetic Energy: \(E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m}\);   Work-Energy Theorem: \(W = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mu^2\).
  • Gravitational Potential Energy: \(E_p = m \times g \times h\).
  • Law of Conservation of Mechanical Energy: \(E_k + E_p = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + mgh = \text{Constant}\).
  • Power: \(P = \frac{\text{Work (W)}}{\text{Time (t)}} = F \times v\)   (SI Unit: \(\text{Watt (W)} = \text{J/s}\);   \(1\text{ kW} = 1000\text{ W}\),   \(1\text{ hp} \approx 746\text{ W}\)).
  • Commercial Unit of Electrical Energy: \(1\text{ Unit} = 1\text{ kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ Joules}\).
  • Simple Machines & Levers: \(\text{Mechanical Advantage (MA)} = \frac{\text{Load (L)}}{\text{Effort (E)}} = \frac{\text{Effort Arm}}{\text{Load Arm}}\);   \(\text{Efficiency } \eta = \frac{\text{Work Output}}{\text{Work Input}} \times 100\%\).
Science • 04
Physics: Sound Waves, Frequency, Echo & SONAR
  • Frequency & Time Period Relation: \(\nu = \frac{1}{T}\) and \(T = \frac{1}{\nu}\)   (Frequency SI Unit: \(\text{Hertz (Hz)} = \text{s}^{-1}\)).
  • Wave Velocity Equation: \(v = \lambda \times \nu = \frac{\lambda}{T}\)   (where \(v =\) wave speed, \(\lambda =\) wavelength, \(\nu =\) frequency).
  • Echo & SONAR Ranging Formula: Total distance travelled by sound \(= 2d = v \times t \implies d = \frac{v \times t}{2}\).
  • Minimum Distance for Distinct Echo in Air: Since persistence of hearing is \(0.1\text{ s}\) and \(v_{\text{air}} \approx 344\text{ m/s}\) at \(22^\circ\text{C}\), \(d_{\min} = \frac{344 \times 0.1}{2} = 17.2\text{ m}\).
  • Frequency Ranges: Infrasonic: \(< 20\text{ Hz}\) • Human Audible Range: \(20\text{ Hz}\text{ to }20,000\text{ Hz}\) (\(20\text{ kHz}\)) • Ultrasonic: \(> 20\text{ kHz}\).
Science • 05
Chemistry: Solution Concentration, Atomic Structure & Mole Concept
  • Mass by Mass % of a Solution: \(\frac{\text{Mass of Solute}}{\text{Mass of Solution}} \times 100 = \frac{\text{Mass of Solute}}{\text{Mass of Solute} + \text{Mass of Solvent}} \times 100\).
  • Mass by Volume % of a Solution: \(\frac{\text{Mass of Solute}}{\text{Volume of Solution}} \times 100\).
  • Volume by Volume % of a Solution: \(\frac{\text{Volume of Solute}}{\text{Volume of Solution}} \times 100\).
  • Temperature Scale Conversions: \(T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15\)   and   \(^\circ\text{F} = \frac{9}{5}(^\circ\text{C}) + 32\).
  • Atomic Number (\(Z\)) & Mass Number (\(A\)): \(Z = \text{Number of protons } (p^+)\);   \(A = \text{Protons } (Z) + \text{Neutrons } (n^0) \implies n^0 = A - Z\).
  • Bohr-Bury Shell Capacity Rule: Maximum electrons in orbit \(n = 2n^2\)   (\(K=2,\; L=8,\; M=18,\; N=32\); outermost shell holds at most \(8\) electrons).
  • Average Atomic Mass of Isotopes: \(M_{\text{avg}} = \frac{(M_1 \times p_1) + (M_2 \times p_2)}{100}\)   (e.g., Chlorine: \(35 \times \frac{75}{100} + 37 \times \frac{25}{100} = 35.5\text{ u}\)).
  • Mole & Avogadro's Constant: \(\text{Number of Moles } (n) = \frac{\text{Given Mass } (m)}{\text{Molar Mass } (M)} = \frac{\text{Number of Particles } (N)}{N_A}\), where \(N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}\).
