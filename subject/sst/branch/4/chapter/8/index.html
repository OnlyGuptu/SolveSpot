CBSE Class 9 Syllabus 10 Verified Q&As Step-by-Step + Hints
Q1

1. Why do you think people's wants keep changing over time? How does this affect production in an economy? Why cannot all our wants be satisfied?

  • Why People's Wants Keep Changing: People's wants change as they grow older, as seasons change, and as their income rises. New inventions (like smartphones), advertisements, and changing fashion or lifestyles also create new wants over time.
  • How Changing Wants Affect Production: Producers and companies have to watch what buyers want and shift their resources (land, labour, and machines) accordingly—stopping the production of outdated items (like typewriters) and producing more of what is currently in demand.
  • Why All Our Wants Cannot Be Satisfied: Human wants are endless, and as soon as one want is fulfilled, another takes its place. However, the resources needed to satisfy them—money, time, land, raw materials, and labour—are limited (scarce).
Q2

2. 'Human wants are unlimited and keep changing'. How do you think this constant desire for more creates pressure on the environment? Can the fulfilment of wants and the extraction of resources be balanced?

  • Pressure on the Environment: When people constantly buy and throw away more goods, factories extract huge amounts of coal, oil, minerals, timber, and water from nature. This leads to deforestation, falling groundwater levels, air and water pollution, and mountains of plastic and electronic waste.
  • Can Wants and Resource Extraction Be Balanced?:
    • Yes, they can be balanced by following sustainable development—meeting our needs today without destroying resources for future generations.
    • We can do this by shifting to renewable energy (solar and wind), practising the 3Rs (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle), avoiding wasteful luxury consumption, and distinguishing between our genuine needs and endless wants.
Q3

3. Can you think of a resource in your region that is scarce but used wastefully? How could it be managed better?

  • Scarce Resource: Clean Fresh Water / Groundwater.
  • How It Is Wasted: Leaving taps running at home, washing cars with hosepipes, leaking municipal water pipes, and flooding fields with excess tube-well water in agriculture.
  • How It Can Be Managed Better:
    • Using drip and sprinkler irrigation on farms so water goes directly to plant roots without wastage.
    • Making rooftop rainwater harvesting compulsory in homes, schools, and apartment buildings to recharge groundwater.
    • Fixing leaking public pipes promptly and recycling treated wastewater for gardening and industry.
Q4

4. Which economic system—market, planned, or mixed—do you think gives people the most freedom? Which economic system is best suited for promoting innovation? Why?

  • System That Gives the Most Freedom: The Market Economy gives individuals and businesses the highest economic freedom—consumers are free to buy whatever they like, and entrepreneurs are free to start businesses, own private property, and decide what to produce.
  • System Best Suited for Innovation: The Market Economy (or the private sector within a Mixed Economy).
  • Why: Because many private companies compete with one another to attract customers and earn profits. This healthy competition pushes businesses to invent better products, improve quality, and use smarter technology.
Q5

5. Critically examine why pure economic systems rarely exist in reality. Assess the limitations of such systems and justify why a mixed economy is often considered a more practical and effective approach in real-world contexts.

  • Drawbacks of a Pure Market Economy: Since private businesses work mainly for profit, they neglect poor citizens who cannot afford high prices, ignore public services that do not bring quick profits (like rural roads or free clinics), and can create unfair monopolies or pollution if left unchecked.
  • Drawbacks of a Pure Planned Economy: When the government controls every factory and shop, there is no competition. This often leads to slow decision-making, poor quality goods, long shortages, and very little choice for consumers.
  • Why a Mixed Economy Is More Practical (e.g., India):
    • A mixed economy combines the best features of both systems—allowing private businesses the freedom to innovate, compete, and create jobs, while the government steps in to regulate unfair practices.
    • At the same time, the government provides essential public goods (defence, roads, government schools, hospitals, and ration support) so that poor and vulnerable families are protected.
Q6

6. A student has ₹100 and must choose between buying a notebook or saving the money for buying a tennis racket later. Which economic concept best explains this situation?

a. Demand

b. Opportunity cost

c. Production

d. Inflation

Correct Option: b. Opportunity cost

Reason: Because the student has only ₹100, choosing to buy the notebook means giving up the next best alternative (saving that ₹100 for the tennis racket). In economics, the value of the next best option that you give up is called the opportunity cost.

Q7

7. How does understanding opportunity cost improve the quality of economic decision-making?

  • Makes Us Think Before Spending: When we realise that every choice has a hidden cost—the alternative we have to give up—we think carefully before spending our limited money or time on impulse buys.
  • Helps Use Scarce Resources Wisely: Businesses and families can compare two options side by side and choose the one that gives the greatest real benefit.
  • Helps Governments Set Priorities: Even governments have limited budgets; understanding opportunity cost helps them weigh whether spending money on a new stadium is worth giving up funds that could have built rural schools or hospitals.
Q8

8. Can effective economic decisions be made without reliable data? Support your answer with an example.

No, good economic decisions cannot be made on guesswork without reliable data.

  • Why Data Is Needed: Accurate numbers—such as population figures, rainfall records, price trends, and employment data—help families, businesses, and governments know the real situation before spending money.
  • Example: If the government wants to distribute free ration grains or open new schools without proper census data on how many families and children live in each district, some areas will get too little grain or too few classrooms while resources go wasted elsewhere.
Q9

9. Analyse how a country's present economic choices can shape its long-term future. Why is it important to consider future consequences while making economic decisions today?

  • Investing in Human Capital and Infrastructure: When a country chooses today to spend on good schools, healthcare, skill training, and clean energy, its youth grow up to become a skilled, healthy workforce that drives strong economic growth for decades.
  • Dangers of Short-Sighted Choices: On the other hand, if a country borrows heavily for wasteful spending or cuts down forests and pollutes rivers for quick profits today, future citizens will suffer from heavy debt, climate disasters, and water scarcity.
  • Fairness to Future Generations: Thinking about future consequences ensures that development is sustainable and leaves a healthy economy and environment for the next generation.
Q10

10. Identify a news article from any newspaper of your choice about a product or commodity (such as vegetables, fruits, fuel, or electronics) where producers or companies are deciding how much to produce or supply. Write 2-3 sentences explaining the example you found and why the production decision was made.

Example: Two-Wheeler Companies Increasing Production of Electric Scooters

A recent newspaper report showed that major Indian two-wheeler companies are setting up new factories to produce more electric scooters (EVs) while slowing down new petrol models. Companies made this production decision because high petrol prices, government EV subsidies, and lower running costs have made buyers shift rapidly toward electric scooters.
