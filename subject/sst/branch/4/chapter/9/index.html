Chapter 9: The Price Puzzle: What Drives the market — Overview & Key Topics

CBSE Class 9 Syllabus 12 Verified Q&As Step-by-Step + Hints
Q1

1. An increase in income always leads to a rise in demand for goods. Defend or refute, giving reasons for the same.

I refute (disagree with) this statement. An increase in income does not always increase the demand for every type of good:

  • Normal Goods: For most everyday and better-quality goods (like fresh fruits, branded clothes, or smartphones), demand increases when a family's income rises.
  • Inferior Goods: For cheaper, low-quality substitutes (like coarse grains, kerosene, or very cheap plastic footwear), demand actually falls when income rises because people can now afford to switch to better-quality items.
  • Basic Necessities: For essentials needed in fixed quantities (like salt or matchboxes), demand stays almost the same even if a person becomes much richer.
Q2

2. If petrol prices double, what happens to:

a. Demand for diesel cars

b. Demand for electric cars

c. Demand for car accessories

d. Demand for public transport

  • a. Demand for diesel cars: Increases. Since diesel cars are a substitute for petrol cars, many buyers will prefer diesel cars to avoid expensive petrol.
  • b. Demand for electric cars: Increases. Electric cars are a substitute that does not need petrol at all, so their demand will rise sharply when petrol becomes costly.
  • c. Demand for car accessories: Decreases. Car accessories (like seat covers and car stereos) are complementary goods used with cars; if fewer people buy and drive petrol cars, the demand for accessories will also fall.
  • d. Demand for public transport: Increases. Buses and metro trains are cheaper substitutes for driving a personal petrol car, so more commuters will switch to public transport.
Q3

3. A farmer traditionally irrigates fields manually (labour-intensive). He installs drip irrigation (a technology upgrade) that reduces water use by 40 per cent and increases yield by 30 per cent. How does this affect:

a. His cost of production

b. His willingness to supply at different prices

c. The overall market supply if many farmers adopt this technology

  • a. His cost of production: Decreases per kg of crop. Because he saves 40% on water and labour while getting 30% more harvest from the same land, the cost of growing each kilogram of crop comes down.
  • b. His willingness to supply at different prices: Increases. Since his cost per kg is lower and his harvest is larger, he earns a better profit and is willing to supply more produce at every price level.
  • c. Overall market supply: Increases significantly. If many farmers across the region use drip irrigation and harvest 30% more crop, the total supply arriving in the market will rise (shifting the market supply curve to the right).
Q4

4. During online festival sales, the prices of many products are very low. Use the concept of demand and supply to explain why the sellers sell at such a low price. What happens to the equilibrium when the price is lowered? Does this benefit only consumers or sellers as well? Explain.

  • Why Sellers Offer Low Prices During Festival Sales: According to the Law of Demand, when the price of a good falls, the quantity demanded rises sharply. Sellers cut prices during festivals to attract millions of buyers and sell huge quantities in a few days.
  • What Happens to Market Equilibrium: When sellers slash prices below the normal equilibrium price, demand shoots up (creating temporary excess demand, which is why popular phones or clothes often go "Out of Stock"), prompting sellers to stock up huge inventories in advance to meet the rush.
  • Who Benefits—Consumers or Sellers?: Both benefit!
    • Consumers benefit because they get clothes, electronics, and household items at big discounts.
    • Sellers also benefit because even though their profit on each single item is smaller, selling a massive number of items clears old stock quickly and brings in huge total revenue and profit.
Q5

5. Suppose the government sets a maximum sale price for an essential vaccine below the market-driven price. What is likely to happen? Choose from the options below and elucidate your point.

a. Surplus | b. Shortage | c. No effect | d. Fall in demand

Correct Option: b. Shortage

Explanation:

  • When the government fixes a legal maximum price below the market equilibrium price, it is called a price ceiling.
  • At this lower price, many more people can afford the vaccine, so the quantity demanded rises.
  • At the same time, vaccine manufacturers earn less per dose and may find it hard to cover costs, so the quantity supplied falls. Because demand becomes much greater than supply, a shortage (and long queues) occurs unless the government also steps in to increase supply.
Q6

6. The government levies higher taxes on products such as tobacco and alcohol to promote healthier choices among citizens. Can you find out other goods where price controls have been set in place? What are the reasons for the same?

  • Other Goods with Government Price Controls:
    • Essential Medicines and Medical Devices (like stents): The government sets a maximum price cap so that life-saving treatment remains affordable for common patients.
    • Food Grains at Ration Shops (PDS) and Cooking Gas (LPG): Provided at controlled or subsidised prices for low-income families.
    • Farm Crops (Minimum Support Price - MSP): The government announces a minimum floor price for crops like wheat and paddy so farmers are not forced to sell at a loss when market prices crash.
    • Fertilizers (like Urea): Sold at a fixed, low price so small farmers can afford farm inputs.
  • Reasons for Setting Price Controls: To make sure poor and middle-class families can afford basic necessities like food and healthcare, to stop sellers from overcharging during emergencies, and to protect farmers' incomes.
Q7

7. Can excessive government regulation hurt markets? Explain with suitable examples.

Yes, while some regulation is necessary, excessive government control can harm markets:

  • Shortages and Black Marketing: If the government forces sellers to sell goods at a price lower than their actual cost of production, producers will stop making the product, leading to market shortages and illegal black-market sales at even higher prices.
  • Delays and Red Tape: Too many complicated licences, permits, and inspections discourage small shopkeepers and new entrepreneurs from starting businesses.
  • Fall in Quality and Innovation: When prices and profits are strictly capped for a long time, companies have little incentive or money left to improve product quality.
  • Example: Before 1991, strict licensing rules in India (the "Licence Raj") limited how many scooters or telephones companies could produce, which meant customers had to wait for years just to buy a scooter or get a telephone connection.
Q8

Q9

9. Visit the nearby vegetable market and try to find answers to the following questions:

a. Who decides the prices of different vegetables in the vegetable market?

b. Sometimes the prices of a few vegetables is too high, and sometimes too low. Why is this?

c. The price of tomatoes is high in the morning and eventually gets lower by the evening. Have you ever noticed this? Comment.

  • a. Who Decides Vegetable Prices: No single vendor fixes the price on their own; daily retail prices depend on the wholesale rate at the local mandi (which is set by how many trucks of vegetables arrive that morning versus how much buyers want) plus transport cost.
  • b. Why Vegetable Prices Sometimes Shoot Up or Crash:
    • Why prices become very high: When heavy rains, floods, or heatwaves damage crops or block highways, supply in the market drops sharply and prices shoot up.
    • Why prices become very low: During peak harvest season, huge quantities of fresh vegetables arrive in the market at the same time (excess supply), pushing prices down.
  • c. Why Tomatoes Are Costlier in the Morning and Cheaper by Evening: Tomatoes are perishable and spoil quickly without cold storage. In the morning, the tomatoes are fresh and many customers are shopping, so vendors sell at a higher price. By late evening, vendors lower their prices so they can sell off their remaining stock instead of letting it rot overnight.
Q10

10. Categorise the following combination of goods into substitute goods and complementary goods:

a. Movie ticket in the cinema hall and popcorn | b. Eraser and pencil | c. Laptop and computer | d. Air Conditioner and cooler | e. Notebook and pen | f. Apple and banana | g. Mobile and earphones

Pair of Goods Category Reason
a. Movie ticket and popcornComplementary GoodsEnjoyed together when watching a movie at the cinema.
b. Eraser and pencilComplementary GoodsUsed together for writing and correcting mistakes.
c. Laptop and computerSubstitute GoodsBoth perform the same computing work and can be used in place of each other.

Q11

11. Fig. 9.8 shows the demand curve DD' and supply curve SS'. Based on the figure, answer the following questions:

a. What does point E represent in this market?

b. What is the equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity at point E?

c. Point A lies on DD'. Point B lies on SS'. What do the points A and B indicate about demand and supply? What does the gap between A and B represent?

d. Point F lies on DD'. Point C lies on SS'. What do the points F and C indicate about demand and supply? What does the gap between C and F represent?

e. If the price stays at the lower dashed line, what could happen next in a free market?

  • a. Point E: Represents Market Equilibrium—the point where the demand curve (DD') and supply curve (SS') intersect, meaning Quantity Demanded equals Quantity Supplied.
  • b. Equilibrium Price and Quantity: Equilibrium Price = ₹250; Equilibrium Quantity = 30 kg.
  • c. Points A, B and the Gap AB: At the higher price of ₹300, Point A shows low quantity demanded while Point B shows high quantity supplied. Because supply is greater than demand, the gap between A and B represents Excess Supply (Surplus).
  • d. Points C, F and the Gap CF: At the lower dashed price line, Point C shows low quantity supplied while Point F shows high quantity demanded. Because demand is greater than supply, the gap between C and F represents Excess Demand (Shortage).
  • e. What Happens Next in a Free Market: Since there is a shortage at the lower price, eager buyers will compete to buy the limited stock, pushing the price upward. As the price rises toward ₹250, sellers will supply more and demand will settle back to equilibrium at Point E.
Q12

12. Draw a market equilibrium graph using the following demand schedule.

Price (₹) 10 20 30 40 50
Q.D. (kg)510152025
Q.S. (kg)252015105

a. Plot the demand and supply curve using the above data.

b. Identify the equilibrium price and quantity.

c. Observe the above data and analyse what happens if the price is set at ₹20 or ₹40.

  • a. Plotting the Curves:
    • Plotting the points from the table (note: in the textbook table, the row labels for Q.D. and Q.S. are swapped—normally Quantity Demanded falls from 25 kg to 5 kg as price rises, while Quantity Supplied rises from 5 kg to 25 kg).
    • The downward-sloping curve shows Demand, and the upward-sloping curve shows Supply.
  • b. Equilibrium Price and Quantity: Both curves meet at Point E, where Quantity Demanded equals Quantity Supplied:
    • Equilibrium Price = ₹30
    • Equilibrium Quantity = 15 kg
  • c. What Happens at ₹20 and ₹40:
    • At ₹20: Quantity demanded (20 kg) is higher than quantity supplied (10 kg), creating a 10 kg shortage (excess demand), which pushes the price up toward ₹30.
    • At ₹40: Quantity supplied (20 kg) is higher than quantity demanded (10 kg), creating a 10 kg surplus (excess supply) of unsold stock, which pushes the price back down toward ₹30.
