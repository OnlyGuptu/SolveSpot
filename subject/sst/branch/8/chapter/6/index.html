Q1

Questions and Activities

1. Explain why a business can fail even with a great idea if one functional area [finance, human resources (HR), operations, or marketing] is weak. If you were running a bakery, which functional area would you prioritise first and why?

1. Why a Business Can Fail If Any One Functional Area Is Weak:

The four functional areas of a business—Finance, Human Resources (HR), Operations, and Marketing—are like the four wheels of a car. Even with a great business idea, if one wheel breaks, the business cannot move forward:

  • Finance: If accounts and cash are poorly managed, the business will run out of money to pay rent, workers, or raw material suppliers.
  • Human Resources (HR): If workers are untrained, rude, or unhappy, product quality and customer service will suffer.
  • Management & Operations: If daily production is messy or delayed, goods will be defective or out of stock when customers ask for them.
  • Marketing: If nobody knows about the product or its price is set wrong, even the best product will remain unsold on the shelf.

2. Which Area I Would Prioritise First in a Bakery:

If I were running a bakery, I would prioritise Management and Operations first. Bread, cakes, and pastries are perishable food items—customers will only trust my bakery if the kitchen is clean, ingredients (flour, butter, milk) are fresh, and every batch comes out of the oven tasting fresh and delicious on time every morning. Once quality and daily operations are strong, word-of-mouth and marketing will naturally bring in more customers.

Q2

Questions and Activities

2. Do you think innovation alone is enough to make a business successful? Why or why not?

No, innovation or a new idea alone is not enough to make a business successful.

While a creative idea helps a startup stand out, turning that idea into a lasting, successful business also requires:

  • Solving a Real Customer Need: The product must be something people actually need and can afford to buy.
  • Good Execution and Quality Control: The entrepreneur must be able to produce the item reliably at a reasonable cost and deliver it on time.
  • Careful Financial Management: Keeping track of costs, pricing, and savings so the business does not run out of cash.
  • Hard Work, Patience, and Resilience: As seen in the stories of Indian entrepreneurs in this chapter, every new business faces initial failures, money shortages, and tough competition—success requires determination and learning from mistakes.
Q3

Questions and Activities

3. "A business plan is a dynamic roadmap for a startup." Explain this statement with suitable examples. When should one change the business plan?

1. Why a Business Plan Is Called a "Dynamic Roadmap":

A business plan is a written guide that sets out what a startup will sell, who its customers are, how the product will be made, and how much money is needed. It is called a dynamic roadmap because it is not fixed in stone—just as a driver changes routes if a road is blocked, an entrepreneur updates the business plan as market conditions change and they learn from real customer feedback.

2. When Should an Entrepreneur Change or Update the Business Plan?:

  • When Customer Tastes Change: For example, if a bakery notices customers asking for sugar-free or millet-based biscuits instead of maida cookies, it should update its menu and plan.
  • When Raw Material Costs Rise or New Competitors Enter: If ingredient prices shoot up or a rival shop opens nearby, pricing and marketing plans need to be adjusted.
  • When New Technology Arrives: For example, a local bookstore or clothing shop updating its business plan to take online orders and accept UPI digital payments.
Q4

Questions and Activities

4. Do you think every entrepreneur should think about the impact of their goods or services on society and the environment? Explain using relevant examples.

Yes, every entrepreneur should care about how their business affects society and the environment. A business does not operate in isolation—it uses natural resources from the environment and depends on the community for workers and customers.

Examples from the Chapter:

  • Positive Social Impact (Shri Mahila Griha Udyog Lijjat Papad): Started in 1959 by seven women in Mumbai with just ₹80, Lijjat Papad grew into a cooperative where tens of thousands of women roll papads from home and earn an honest, dignified livelihood as equal partners (sahodaris).
  • Positive Environmental Impact (Eco-Friendly Startups): Startups that make biodegradable jute/cloth bags, bamboo products, or solar lamps help reduce plastic pollution and protect nature while earning a fair profit.
  • Why It Matters: Businesses that treat workers fairly, avoid polluting rivers and air, and sell safe products win long-term customer trust and help the whole nation prosper.
Q5

Questions and Activities

5. In 1931, two brothers, Digambar and Govind Dandekar, started a small business (Camlin / Camel art supplies)...

a. What entrepreneurial qualities did the Dandekar brothers display?
b. What opportunity did they identify in the market?
c. Was travelling to Glasgow a risky decision?
d. Which functional area helped the company improve its products?
e. How did they serve society apart from earning profits?

  1. Entrepreneurial Qualities Displayed: Determination, resilience (not giving up after early failures), willingness to learn, hard work, and a strong focus on product quality.
  2. Market Opportunity Identified: They noticed that in pre-independence India, good-quality writing inks, watercolours, and art materials were not made locally and had to be imported from abroad at high prices.
  3. Was Travelling to Glasgow Risky?: Yes, spending their limited money to travel all the way to Glasgow (Scotland) was a big financial risk, but it was a smart, calculated risk because learning modern colour chemistry was essential to fix their product quality.
  4. Functional Area That Helped Improve Products: Management and Operations (Research & Quality Control)—setting up a proper laboratory and testing ingredients carefully helped them make colours suited to Indian weather.
  5. How They Served Society: They made affordable, high-quality Swadeshi pens, inks, geometry boxes, and colours (Camel/Camlin) available to millions of Indian school children and artists, reducing India's dependence on foreign imports and creating local jobs.
Q6

Questions and Activities

6. How do you think entrepreneurship is important for India's goal of becoming a developed nation by 2047?

Entrepreneurship plays a key role in helping India become a developed, self-reliant nation (Viksit Bharat by 2047):

  • Creates Jobs for Millions of Youth: Instead of only looking for jobs, entrepreneurs become job creators by starting small businesses, factories, and startups that employ millions of people.
  • Solves Local Indian Problems: Indian startups in agriculture (Agri-Tech), healthcare, clean energy, and digital payments (FinTech) create affordable solutions for farmers and small towns.
  • Reduces Dependence on Imports (Atmanirbhar Bharat): When Indian entrepreneurs manufacture electronics, medicines, toys, and machines within the country, India saves foreign exchange and exports goods to the world.
  • Boosts National Income and Infrastructure: Successful businesses increase GDP and pay taxes that help the government build better roads, schools, railways, and hospitals.
Q7

Questions and Activities

7. Choose a product or service and develop a simple business plan.

Simple Business Plan: "GreenCarry" – Eco-Friendly Jute & Cloth Shopping Bags

Business Plan Part My Plan
1. Business Idea & Product Washable, strong, and attractive jute and cotton shopping bags with compartments for vegetables and groceries, replacing single-use plastic bags.
2. Target Customers Local households, weekly vegetable shoppers, grocery stores, and gift shops in our town.
3. Operations & Human Resources Buy jute and cotton cloth in bulk from wholesale markets and partner with 4–5 local tailors and women's self-help groups to stitch and print the bags.
4. Finance (Cost & Price) Cost per bag (cloth + stitching): ₹30
Selling Price: ₹45 per bag
Profit per bag: ₹15
5. Marketing Plan Set up a weekend stall near the local vegetable market, tie up with local grocery shops to keep our bags at the billing counter, and offer custom name printing for bulk orders.

Questions and Activities

9. Take any popular brand and identify:
• The problem it tackles
• Its initial target customers
• How it differentiated itself
• The resources it may have started with

Case Study of a Popular Indian Brand: Shri Mahila Griha Udyog Lijjat Papad

Point Details
The Problem It Tackles Many homemakers from low-income families had cooking skills (like rolling papads) but lacked a way to earn an independent, respectable income from home, while families wanted clean, tasty homemade papads.
Initial Target Customers Local families and neighbourhood grocery shopkeepers in Girgaum, Mumbai.
How It Differentiated Itself Strict quality control (using the exact same kneaded dough so every papad tasted crisp and identical) and a unique cooperative model where every woman worker is a co-owner (sister / sahodari) who shares the profits equally.
Resources It Started With Started in 1959 on a building terrace in Mumbai by seven women with a borrowed seed loan of just ₹80, basic urad dal flour, spices, rolling pins, and a charcoal stove.
