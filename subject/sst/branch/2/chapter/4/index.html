CBSE Class 9 Syllabus 13 Verified Q&As Step-by-Step + Hints
Q1

1. Do you think life became easier or more challenging after humans started farming? Give two reasons for your answer.

  • How Life Became Easier (Food Security): Growing crops and keeping domestic animals gave people a steady, reliable food supply in one place. They no longer had to risk their lives every day wandering in search of wild animals and plants.
  • How Life Became More Challenging (Hard Labour and Crop Risks): Farming required hard physical work throughout the year—clearing land, ploughing, sowing, weeding, and watering crops. Also, since farmers stayed in one place, a single drought, flood, or crop disease could wipe out their harvest and cause starvation.
Q2

2. The environment offers human societies both opportunities as well as challenges. Explain with reference to early farming communities and river-valley civilisations.

  • Opportunities Provided by Rivers: River valleys such as the Indus-Sarasvatī, Nile, Tigris-Euphrates, and Huang He gave early farmers year-round fresh water, rich alluvial soil deposited by annual floods, and easy boat transport for trade.
  • Challenges Faced by Communities: At the same time, sudden heavy floods could destroy villages and standing crops, while years of low rainfall caused severe droughts. To overcome these problems, early societies learned to build irrigation canals, reservoirs, and flood embankments.
Q3

3. Why do historians divide early human history into different ages such as Stone Age, Bronze Age, and Iron Age? What does this classification tell us about human progress?

  • Why History is Divided into These Ages: Historians name these periods after the main material people used to make their tools, weapons, and everyday implements during that time.
  • What It Tells Us About Human Progress: This division shows how human technology improved step by step—from chipping rough stones in the Stone Age, to melting and mixing copper and tin in the Bronze Age, and finally smelting strong iron tools in the Iron Age, which made it possible to clear dense forests, plough harder soil, and build large cities.
Q4

4. Imagine you are a Neolithic farmer. Describe one day of your life. What challenges would you face that a hunter-gatherer would not?

  • A Day in the Life of a Neolithic Farmer: I wake up at dawn in my mud-and-thatch hut, milk and feed the goats and cattle, spend the morning loosening the soil and pulling weeds in our wheat and barley fields using polished stone tools, grind grain on a flat grinding stone (quern), and help bake clay pots for storing grain.
  • Challenges Compared to Hunter-Gatherers: Unlike hunter-gatherers who can simply move to another forest if food runs short, my family is tied to our village fields. We constantly have to protect our ripened crops from wild animals and birds, and if the rains fail, we face the danger of hunger.
Q5

5. Imagine that the Harappan script gets deciphered tomorrow. What new types of information do you think historians might learn?

  • Who Ruled the Harappan Cities: Reading the script would finally tell us how Harappan cities were governed—whether by kings, priests, or councils of merchants—and what laws kept their town planning and weights so uniform.
  • Religious Beliefs, Names, and Trade Records: We would learn the actual names of Harappan rulers and cities, the meaning of the symbols and animals carved on their seals, their prayers and stories, and details of their trade agreements with Mesopotamia.
Q6

6. Prepare a table with three columns—Palaeolithic, Mesolithic, and Neolithic and fill in their distinctive features: tools, settlements, art, and subsistence.

Feature Palaeolithic Age Mesolithic Age Neolithic Age
Tools Large, rough stone handaxes, cleavers, and choppers Microliths (tiny, sharp stone blades fixed onto wood or bone handles) Smooth, polished stone axes, sickles, and grinding stones (querns)
Settlements Nomadic life; lived in natural caves, rock shelters, and open camps Semi-nomadic; stayed in seasonal camps and rock shelters Settled village life in permanent mud-brick and thatched huts
Art Early cave markings, simple bone and shell beads Lively cave and rock paintings of hunting and dancing (e.g., Bhimbetka) Painted clay pottery, terracotta figurines, and stone/shell ornaments
Subsistence (Food) Hunting large wild animals and gathering wild fruits, roots, and nuts Hunting smaller animals, fishing, and collecting wild grains Growing crops (wheat, barley, rice) and rearing domesticated animals
Q7

7. "Bronze Age civilisations developed independently but shared common features." Examine this statement with reference to the civilisations given in the chapter.

  • Independent Growth in Different River Valleys: Each Bronze Age civilisation grew in its own river basin—the Sindhu-Sarasvatī (Harappan) in India and Pakistan, Mesopotamia along the Tigris and Euphrates, Egypt along the Nile, and China along the Huang He (Yellow River).
  • Common Features Shared by All: Despite developing in different regions, all of them depended on fertile river plains and irrigation to grow surplus food, knew how to smelt copper and bronze for tools, built well-planned cities with grand monuments, carried out long-distance trade, and invented scripts to keep written records.
Q8

8. Although rivers provided many benefits, they also created challenges for early societies. Discuss both the advantages and disadvantages of settling near rivers.

  • Advantages of Settling Near Rivers: Rivers supplied fresh water for drinking and irrigation, spread fertile silt across the fields during annual floods (such as the rich black silt called kemet along the Nile), provided fish, and acted as natural waterways for boats carrying trade goods.
  • Disadvantages and Risks: Heavy, unexpected floods could wash away entire villages and drown crops, while years of low water caused droughts and famine. Sometimes rivers even changed their course or dried up, forcing people to abandon their cities.
Q9

9. With the help of your teacher, find out more about the Code of Hammurabi. Why was it important? Do you think it was fair to all sections of society? Give reasons for your answer.

  • Why the Code of Hammurabi Was Important: Issued by King Hammurabi of Babylon around 1792 BCE and carved onto a tall stone pillar, it is one of the world's oldest and most complete written codes of law, covering trade, property, family life, and crime.
  • Was It Fair to Everyone?: No, it was not equally fair to all classes. Although it protected the weak from some abuses, the punishment for a crime depended on a person's social rank—offences against nobles were punished much more harshly (often "an eye for an eye") than the same offences committed against commoners or slaves.
Q10

10. If you had to choose one major innovation from early civilisations that changed the world permanently, what would it be and why?

  • Chosen Innovation: The invention of Writing (such as Mesopotamian cuneiform, Egyptian hieroglyphs, and the Harappan script).
  • Why It Changed the World: Before writing, knowledge could only be passed down by word of mouth and was easily forgotten. Writing allowed humans to store laws, trade accounts, scientific ideas, and literature permanently so future generations could read and build upon them.
Q11

11. Compare the social hierarchy and daily life of people in the Egyptian civilisation with those in Mesopotamia or China. What similarities and differences do you notice?

  • Similarities: All three societies had a pyramid-like social structure with a powerful ruler at the top (the Pharaoh in Egypt, the King in Mesopotamia, and the Shang/Zhou King in China), followed by priests, nobles, and scribes, while farmers, craftspeople, and labourers made up the largest group at the bottom.
  • Differences: In Egypt, religious life focused heavily on preparing for the afterlife by preserving bodies (mummies) and building giant stone pyramids, and Egyptian women enjoyed greater rights to own property. In contrast, Mesopotamian city life revolved around stepped mud-brick temples called ziggurats, while early China placed special importance on ancestor worship, bronze vessels, and predicting the future using oracle bones.
Q12

12. Activity: Using maps, locate the major rivers and civilisations of Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, and the Sindhu-Sarasvatī Valley. Mark the trade links between them.

  • Major Civilisations and Their Rivers:
    • Egyptian Civilisation: Along the Nile River in north-eastern Africa.
    • Mesopotamian Civilisation: Between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers (modern Iraq).
    • Sindhu-Sarasvatī (Harappan) Civilisation: Along the Indus and Sarasvatī river basins in north-western India and Pakistan.
    • Chinese Civilisation: Along the Huang He (Yellow River) and Yangtze River.
  • Trade Links: Harappan merchants sailed from ports like Lothal across the Arabian Sea and Persian Gulf to trade with Mesopotamia (where the Indus region was called Meluhha), stopping at trading ports in Oman (Magan) and Bahrain (Dilmun) to exchange carnelian beads, cotton textiles, ivory, and timber for copper and silver.
Q13

13. Activity: Choose one early civilisation (Mesopotamia, Egypt, or China) and prepare a mini-scrapbook or a presentation showing their innovations in tools, writing, art, and architecture. Include pictures, brief descriptions, and explain their significance.

  • Chosen Civilisation: Mesopotamia (Sumer, Akkad, Babylon).
  • Writing (Cuneiform): The world's earliest wedge-shaped script on damp clay tablets, enabling the recording of the Epic of Gilgamesh, administration, and trade accounts.
  • Technology & Mathematics: Invented wheeled transport carts, sailboats, canal irrigation systems, the base-60 numbering system, the 60-minute hour, and the 360-degree circle.
  • Architecture (Ziggurats): Grand stepped mud-brick tower temples that functioned as holy sanctums, public treasuries, and administrative nerve centers.
  • Law & Art (Code of Hammurabi): Carved basalt stone stelae depicting King Hammurabi receiving laws from the justice god Shamash, establishing codified civil standards.
