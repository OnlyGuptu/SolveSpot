CBSE Class 9 Syllabus 15 Verified Q&As Step-by-Step + Hints
Q1

1. How did political organisation change from the Vedic period to the age of large empires such as the Mauryas and the Guptas? Explain the administrative system of the early Indian states.

  • Growth from Clans to Empires: In the Vedic period, people were organised into kinship-based clans or tribes called janas, led by a rājā. As iron tools and settled farming spread, these clans settled into fixed territories called janapadas, which later grew into sixteen powerful kingdoms and republics known as mahājanapadas. Eventually, states like Magadha expanded to form huge empires under the Mauryas and the Guptas.
  • Administrative System: The king was the head of the state and was advised by a council of ministers (mantri-parishad). To govern large territories smoothly, empires were divided into provinces (called bhuktis in the north or maṇḍalams in the south), which were further divided into districts (vishayas or nādus). At the grassroots level, villages were managed by a village headman (grāmika) and local village councils.
Q2

2. Describe the role of the king, important officers, and the methods used to govern large territories.

  • Role of the King: The king's main duties (rājadharma) were to protect the kingdom from external attacks, maintain law and order, deliver fair justice, and look after the welfare of the people by building roads, rest houses, and irrigation tanks.
  • Important Officers: The king governed with the help of senior ministers and officials called amātyas, army commanders (senāpati), revenue collectors, treasurers, and ministers for peace and foreign affairs (such as the sāndhivigrahika in the Gupta period).
  • Methods Used to Govern Large Territories: Rulers divided the empire into provinces and districts under royal princes or governors (pradeśhikas), allowed local village assemblies and merchant guilds to manage their own local affairs, and built royal highways to keep communication open across the empire.
Q3

3. After studying this chapter, what do you think were the most important features of the state and society in India before 1000 CE?

  • Rule Guided by Dharma: Kings were not expected to rule according to personal whims; both rulers and citizens were guided by moral duty (dharma), natural order (ṛita), and the welfare of all living beings.
  • Strong Local Self-Government: Even when dynasties changed at the top, daily life remained stable because autonomous village councils (like the sabha and ur) and craft/merchant guilds (shreṇīs) managed local administration, taxes, and justice on their own.
  • Flourishing Trade and Learning: India was connected by major trade routes like the Uttarāpatha and Dakshiṇāpatha as well as sea routes, while great centres of learning like Takshaśhilā, Nālandā, and Vikramśhilā attracted students from across Asia.
Q4

4. What do early texts such as the Rigveda, Arthashastra, and the Mahābhārata reveal about political and social life?

  • Rigveda: Shows an early pastoral and agricultural society where the rājā consulted popular assemblies like the sabhā, samiti, and vidatha, and where occupations were flexible rather than fixed by birth.
  • Arthashastra (by Kauṭilya): Gives a detailed practical guide on how to run a state, explaining the seven limbs of a state (Saptāṅga), taxation, law courts, spy systems, defense, and public welfare.
  • Mahābhārata: Particularly in the Shānti Parva, it teaches that a king's highest duty is rājadharma—protecting the weak, ruling justly without cruelty, and putting the people's welfare above his own desires.
Q5

5. What can we learn from early Indian society about varna and the role of women?

  • The Varna System: In the early Vedic period, the four varṇas (Brāhmaṇa, Kshatriya, Vaishya, and Shūdra) were based on a person's occupation and qualities rather than birth, and people could change their work. Over later centuries, however, the system gradually became hereditary and rigid.
  • Role of Women: In early times, women received education, took part in religious debates, and composed Vedic hymns (such as Lopāmudrā, Gārgī, and Maitreyī). Although social rules became stricter in later periods, women continued to work in agriculture and crafts, royal women like Prabhāvatī Gupta ruled as regents, and many women in north and south India were celebrated as poets, scholars, and temple donors.
Q6

6. Explain how assemblies like sabhā and samiti limited the power of the rājā. Which modern institutions perform similar functions today?

  • How Sabhā and Samiti Checked the Rājā's Power: In early India, the sabhā (a smaller council of elders and wise members) and the samiti (a larger general assembly of the clan) met to discuss war, peace, justice, and even the selection of the ruler. Because the rājā needed their support and advice, he could not act like an unchecked dictator.
  • Modern Equivalents: Today, the Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha), State Legislative Assemblies, the Judiciary, and village Gram Sabhas perform a similar role by debating laws and questioning the government.
Q7

7. What do the terms varṇa and jāti refer to in early Indian society? How were they different, and what factors may have contributed to the formation of various jātis?

  • Difference Between Varṇa and Jāti: Varṇa refers to the broad four-fold theoretical classification mentioned in ancient texts (priests/teachers, warriors/rulers, farmers/traders, and workers/artisans). Jāti refers to the hundreds of smaller, local, birth-based social groups linked to specific crafts or regions.
  • How Various Jātis Formed: New jātis emerged over time as specialised occupational guilds (like weavers, potters, or smiths) became close-knit communities, as forest-dwelling tribes joined settled agricultural society, and through migration and intermarriage across groups.
Q8

8. Why do you think education in early India emphasised both knowledge and moral values? How might this have benefited society?

  • Why Both Were Emphasised: Ancient Indian teachers believed that book knowledge or skill without good character could be misused. Therefore, students in gurukulas and vihāras were taught self-discipline, truthfulness, humility, and respect for nature alongside subjects like mathematics, grammar, medicine, and philosophy.
  • How It Benefited Society: It produced honest officials, fair judges, ethical merchants, and caring doctors who used their education to serve the community rather than just for personal greed.
Q9

9. Look at the major trade routes of early India (Fig 5.12). How do you think these routes helped people in the exchange of goods, skills, beliefs, and cultural practices?

  • Exchange of Goods and Craft Skills: Major overland highways like the Uttarāpatha (connecting the northwest to the Ganga plains) and Dakshiṇāpatha (linking north India to the peninsula) connected inland towns with busy seaports, allowing merchants and artisans to trade cotton textiles, spices, iron tools, horses, and precious stones.
  • Spread of Ideas, Beliefs, and Culture: Monks, scholars, pilgrims, and storytellers travelled alongside merchant caravans along these same roads, spreading Buddhism, Jainism, Vedic traditions, art styles, languages, and medical knowledge across India and beyond to Central and Southeast Asia.
Q10

10. What might have been the advantages and challenges of ruling a large empire in the absence of modern communication systems?

  • Advantages of a Large Empire: The ruler collected higher tax revenues from rich agricultural lands and trade routes, commanded a large army, and maintained peace across a vast region, which helped trade prosper.
  • Challenges Without Modern Communication: Messages sent by horse riders or runners took weeks to reach distant provinces. Because of this delay, governors in faraway provinces could easily rebel or misuse their power, and defending long borders was expensive and difficult.
Q11

11. Many ideas about governance come from texts composed by scholars and advisors of the king. What might be some limitations of relying only on such sources?

  • Shows Rules Rather Than Real Life: Books written by court scholars (like the Arthashastra or Dharmashāstras) often describe how a kingdom ought to work in theory, not necessarily how rules were actually followed on the ground.
  • One-Sided Royal Viewpoint: Court poets and advisors naturally praised their patron kings and focused on palace life, leaving out the everyday struggles and viewpoints of ordinary peasants, artisans, women, and labourers. Historians therefore cross-check these texts with inscriptions, coins, and archaeological findings.
Q12

12. Read the source and answer the questions (Nashik cave inscription of Ushavadāta):
a. What does this source tell us about the economic role of guilds?
b. Why were guilds trusted with money deposits?
c. Identify the donor and the donees from the given source.

  • a. Economic Role of Guilds: The inscription shows that craft guilds (shreṇīs), such as the weavers' guilds of Govardhana (Nashik), acted like modern banks—they accepted permanent cash deposits, invested the money in their trade, and paid regular interest to fund charitable work.
  • b. Why Guilds Were Trusted: Guilds were stable, well-organised associations with strict internal rules and high reputation. Even if a king or dynasty changed, the guild continued its business reliably and honoured its financial commitments.
  • c. Donor and Donees: The donor was Ushavadāta (son-in-law of the ruler Nahapāna), and the donees (beneficiaries) were the Buddhist monks living in the Nashik caves, who received new robes and food expenses from the interest on the deposit.
Q13

13. Mark and locate on the map of India the following important centres: Pāṭaliputra, Nāśhik, Ujjayinī, Vikramśhila, Kānchipuram, Mathurā, Rājgriha.

  • Location Guide for the Outline Map of India:
    • Pāṭaliputra: Modern Patna, on the southern bank of the Ganga in Bihar.
    • Nāśhik: In north-western Maharashtra, near the source of the Godavari River.
    • Ujjayinī: Modern Ujjain in western Madhya Pradesh (Malwa region).
    • Vikramśhila: Near Kahalgaon in Bhagalpur district, eastern Bihar.
    • Kānchipuram: In northern Tamil Nadu, near Chennai.
    • Mathurā: On the banks of the Yamuna River in western Uttar Pradesh.
    • Rājgriha: Modern Rajgir in Nalanda district, Bihar (south of Pāṭaliputra).
Q14

14. Prepare a short presentation or poster on one of the following: a. Life in the Vedic society, b. Early education system (gurukula), c. Trade and guilds in early India, d. Role of women in early Indian society.

  • The Guru-Shishya Bond: Students lived in the āśhrama as members of the teacher's family, sharing daily chores, living simply, and learning discipline and mutual respect regardless of their social background.
  • Wide Range of Subjects: Alongside the Vedas, grammar, logic, and philosophy, students learned practical subjects like mathematics, astronomy, medicine (Ayurveda), statecraft, and archery.
  • Focus on Character and Values: Education was not just for earning a living—it aimed to build truthfulness, self-control, gratitude, and a sense of duty toward society and nature.
Q15

15. Divide the class into three groups. Each group will work on one project (water, wind, and glacier). The project should highlight the causes, impact on human life and the environment, and mitigation measures.

  • Group 1 – Running Water: Heavy rains and river floods erode soil and damage riverside villages and crops; this can be reduced by building embankments, check dams, and planting trees along riverbanks.
  • Group 2 – Wind: Strong desert winds blow away dry topsoil and cause sandstorms; this can be controlled by planting shelterbelts of trees, fixing sand dunes with bushes, and avoiding overgrazing.
  • Group 3 – Glaciers: Melting glaciers can trigger avalanches and sudden glacial lake floods (GLOFs) in mountain valleys; impacts can be reduced through satellite monitoring, early warning alarms, and draining excess water from glacial lakes.
