Q1

Questions and Activities

1. Why does India have diverse biomes? How does the local geography connect to the diversity?

India has a wide variety of biomes because its physical geography—mountains, plateaus, plains, deserts, and coasts—creates very different temperatures, altitudes, soil types, and rainfall patterns across the country:

  • High Mountains (The Himalayas): Freezing temperatures and high altitudes create alpine meadows and coniferous forests, home to animals with thick fur like the snow leopard and red panda.
  • Heavy Rainfall Regions (Western Ghats, North-East India, and Andaman & Nicobar Islands): Warm temperatures and heavy monsoon rains support dense, evergreen Tropical Rainforest biomes rich in plant and animal species.
  • Dry and Arid Regions (Western India): Very low rainfall in Rajasthan creates the hot, sandy Thar Desert biome with thorny bushes and camels, while the Rann of Kutch in Gujarat forms a unique salt-desert ecosystem.
Q2

Questions and Activities

2. Make a list of economically valuable native plants in your region and comment on their abundance.

Examples of economically valuable native plants found across Indian regions and their abundance:

  • Neem, Tulsi, and Amla (Found across most Indian plains): Very common and abundant; widely used in households, traditional Ayurvedic medicines, soaps, and health products.
  • Bamboo and Teak/Sal Timber (Assam, Tripura, Kerala, and Central India): Abundant in moist deciduous and evergreen belts; used for furniture, house construction, paper, and handicrafts.
  • Mahua, Tendu, and Sal (Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Jharkhand): Moderately abundant in central Indian forests; tribal communities collect mahua flowers, sal seeds, and tendu leaves as an important source of seasonal income.
  • Medicinal Herbs like Ashwagandha and Brahmi (Himalayas & Western Ghats): Valuable for medicines, though some wild varieties are becoming scarce due to over-collection and now need careful cultivation.
Q3

Questions and Activities

3. Prepare a map of your state or Union Territory showing national parks and sanctuaries.

Students can mark the national parks and wildlife sanctuaries of their own state on an outline map. Some famous protected areas and biosphere reserves across India mentioned in the chapter include:

  • Namdapha National Park (Arunachal Pradesh)
  • Kutch Biosphere Reserve / Wild Ass Sanctuary (Gujarat)
  • Gahirmatha Marine Sanctuary (Odisha – famous for Olive Ridley sea turtles)
  • Gulf of Mannar Marine National Park (Tamil Nadu)
  • Pachmarhi Biosphere Reserve (Madhya Pradesh)
  • Achanakmar-Amarkantak Biosphere Reserve (Madhya Pradesh & Chhattisgarh)
Q4

Questions and Activities

4. Study the impact of forest fires on people dependent on forests. Try to include the causes, losses, and preventive measures.

  • Causes of Forest Fires: Forest fires are caused both by natural conditions—such as extreme summer heatwaves, prolonged dry spells, and lightning—and by human carelessness, such as throwing lit matchsticks/cigarettes, leaving campfires burning, or uncontrolled burning of dry grass.
  • Losses & Impact on Forest-Dependent People: Fires destroy valuable minor forest produce (honey, mahua flowers, tendu leaves, fruits, firewood, and medicinal herbs) that tribal and rural families depend on for food and income. Smoke causes breathing illnesses, livestock lose grazing pastures, and families living near forest edges risk losing their huts.
  • Preventive Measures: Clearing dry leaf litter along fire-lines before summer, strict rules against lighting open fires in forests, using satellite alerts from the Forest Survey of India (FSI), and involving local village Gram Sabhas (as in Mendha Lekha village) to guard and put out small fires immediately.
Q5

Questions and Activities

5. Why are forests often called the 'lungs of the Earth'?

Forests—especially dense tropical rainforests—are called the 'lungs of the Earth' because, during photosynthesis, millions of trees take in huge amounts of carbon dioxide from the air and release fresh oxygen that humans and animals need to breathe. Just as our lungs clean our blood and keep us alive with oxygen, forests purify the Earth's atmosphere and help control global warming.

Q6

Questions and Activities

6. If large forest areas continue to be destroyed, what do you think would be its long-term effect on the global climate?

If large forest areas continue to be cut down, there will be far fewer trees left to absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. This build-up of greenhouse gases will speed up global warming and climate change, leading to hotter temperatures, melting glaciers, irregular monsoon rains, more frequent droughts and floods, loss of fertile soil, and the extinction of many plant and animal species.

Q7

Questions and Activities

7. Discuss some traditional forest conservation methods with your grandparents or an elderly person.

Traditional Indian methods of protecting forests and trees passed down by elders include:

  • Sacred Groves: Across India, villagers have protected untouched patches of forest dedicated to local deities—known as Devrai in Maharashtra, Devarakadu in Karnataka, Kavu in Kerala, Orans in Rajasthan, and Sarna in Jharkhand/Chhattisgarh—where cutting trees or hunting is strictly forbidden.
  • Worshipping Sacred Trees and Nature: Trees like Peepal, Banyan, Neem, Khejri, and Tulsi are treated as sacred and protected around villages and water ponds.
  • Sustainable Harvesting Rules: Tribal communities like the Gonds and Bhils follow traditional customs where they take only dry fallen wood or seasonal fruits without harming young trees, and perform thanksgiving rituals to nature before harvesting.
Q8

Questions and Activities

8. How do forest dwellers contribute to the protection and conservation of forests?

Forest dwellers and tribal communities play a vital role in protecting forests:

  • Deep Traditional Knowledge: Having lived in harmony with forests for generations, they know how to collect herbs, fruits, and bamboo sustainably without damaging the forest's growth.
  • Leading Forest Protection Movements: Local communities have bravely led historic movements to save trees—such as the Bishnoi movement in Rajasthan led by Amrita Devi to protect Khejri trees, and the Chipko movement in Uttarakhand where village women hugged trees to stop loggers.
  • Community Forest Management: Through village Gram Sabhas (such as in Mendha Lekha in Maharashtra), villagers patrol the forest, stop illegal logging, prevent forest fires, and manage bamboo harvesting responsibly.
Q9

Questions and Activities

9. What is a forest fire? Prepare a plan for prevention and mitigation of forest fires.

A forest fire (or wildfire) is an uncontrolled fire that spreads rapidly through dry grass, bushes, and trees in a forest, destroying wildlife habitats, timber, and forest resources.

Plan for Prevention and Mitigation of Forest Fires:

  • Creating Fire-Lines: Before the dry summer season begins, clear strips of dry leaves and bushes (fire-lines) across the forest so that if a fire starts in one block, it cannot easily jump to the next.
  • Strict Rules on Human Activities: Ban campfires, smoking, and burning of waste or dry farm stubble near forest boundaries during hot, windy months.
  • Early Warning & Community Patrols: Use satellite fire-alert maps from the Forest Survey of India (FSI) and form joint watch teams of forest guards and local village youth (Gram Sabhas) to spot smoke early and put out small fires before they spread.
